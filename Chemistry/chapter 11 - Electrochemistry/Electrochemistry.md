# Electrochemistry 


## 1. Electrochemistry — Introduction

Electrochemistry is the branch of chemistry that deals with the relationship between chemical energy and electrical energy.

It mainly involves two types of processes:

- Chemical energy to electrical energy: Occurs in galvanic or voltaic cells.
    
- Electrical energy to chemical energy: Occurs in electrolytic cells.
    

### Redox Reactions

Electrochemical processes are based on oxidation-reduction (redox) reactions.

- Oxidation: Loss of electrons.
    
- Reduction: Gain of electrons.
    
- Oxidising agent: Accepts electrons and gets reduced.
    
- Reducing agent: Donates electrons and gets oxidised.
    

Example:

`Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)`

- Zinc loses electrons and is oxidised.
    
- Copper ions gain electrons and are reduced.
    
- Zinc acts as the reducing agent.
    
- Copper ions act as the oxidising agent.
    

Half-reactions:

- Oxidation: `Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻`
    
- Reduction: `Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)`
    

## 2. Electrochemical Cells

An electrochemical cell is a device in which a redox reaction is used to convert chemical energy into electrical energy or electrical energy into chemical energy.

There are two main types.

### Galvanic or Voltaic Cell

A galvanic cell converts the chemical energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy.

Characteristics:

- The redox reaction is spontaneous.
    
- Oxidation occurs at the anode.
    
- Reduction occurs at the cathode.
    
- Electrons flow through the external circuit from anode to cathode.
    
- The anode is negative and the cathode is positive.
    

### Electrolytic Cell

An electrolytic cell uses electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous chemical reaction.

Characteristics:

- The reaction is non-spontaneous under the applied conditions.
    
- Oxidation occurs at the anode.
    
- Reduction occurs at the cathode.
    
- The anode is positive and the cathode is negative.
    

### Difference Between Galvanic and Electrolytic Cells

|Property|Galvanic Cell|Electrolytic Cell|
|---|---|---|
|Energy conversion|Chemical to electrical|Electrical to chemical|
|Reaction|Spontaneous|Non-spontaneous|
|Anode|Negative|Positive|
|Cathode|Positive|Negative|
|Oxidation|At anode|At anode|
|Reduction|At cathode|At cathode|
|Electron flow externally|Anode to cathode|Driven by the external power source|

Important: Oxidation always occurs at the anode and reduction always occurs at the cathode. The signs of the electrodes depend on the type of cell.

## 3. Daniell Cell

A Daniell cell is a galvanic cell in which zinc and copper electrodes are immersed in solutions containing their respective ions.

It converts the chemical energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy.

### Construction

The Daniell cell consists of:

- A zinc rod dipped in zinc sulphate solution.
    
- A copper rod dipped in copper sulphate solution.
    
- A salt bridge connecting the two solutions.
    
- An external wire connecting the two electrodes.
    

### Working

At the zinc electrode (anode):

Zinc undergoes oxidation and releases electrons.

`Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻`

- The zinc electrode gradually loses mass.
    
- The concentration of zinc ions in the solution increases.
    

At the copper electrode (cathode):

Copper ions undergo reduction by accepting electrons.

`Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)`

- Copper gets deposited on the copper electrode.
    
- The concentration of copper ions in the solution decreases.
    

### Overall Cell Reaction

`Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)`

### Cell Representation

The Daniell cell is represented as:

`Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)`

Meaning of the symbols:

- `|` represents a phase boundary.
    
- `||` represents the salt bridge.
    
- The anode is written on the left.
    
- The cathode is written on the right.
    

### Functions of a Salt Bridge

A salt bridge is a tube containing an electrolyte, usually an inert salt such as KCl or KNO₃, in a suitable medium.

Its functions are:

1. Completes the electrical circuit by allowing ions to migrate.
    
2. Maintains electrical neutrality in both half-cells.
    
3. Prevents the direct mixing of the two electrolyte solutions.
    
4. Minimises liquid junction potential.
    

Ion movement in the Daniell cell:

- Anions from the salt bridge migrate towards the anode compartment, where positive ions are being produced.
    
- Cations from the salt bridge migrate towards the cathode compartment, where positive ions are being consumed.
    

Important: Electrons travel through the external wire, whereas ions travel through the electrolyte and salt bridge.

## 4. Electrode Potential

Electrode potential is the potential difference developed between an electrode and the electrolyte in which it is immersed due to the tendency of the electrode species to undergo oxidation or reduction.

When a metal is dipped into a solution containing its ions, two competing processes may occur:

- Metal atoms may lose electrons and enter the solution as positive ions.
    
- Positive ions from the solution may gain electrons and deposit as metal atoms.
    

An equilibrium may develop between these processes.

### Development of Electrode Potential

For a metal M:

`M(s) ⇌ Mⁿ⁺(aq) + ne⁻`

The tendency of an electrode to undergo reduction is expressed through its reduction potential.

A higher reduction potential indicates a greater tendency to undergo reduction relative to the reference electrode under the specified conditions.

### Oxidation Potential and Reduction Potential

- Reduction potential: Tendency to gain electrons.
    
- Oxidation potential: Tendency to lose electrons.
    

For the same electrode reaction under identical conditions:

`E°oxidation = −E°reduction`

Important: Electrode potential cannot be measured independently as an absolute value. It is measured relative to a reference electrode.

## 5. Standard Electrode Potential

Standard electrode potential, represented by E°, is the electrode potential measured under standard conditions relative to the standard hydrogen electrode.

### Standard Conditions

- Solute activities are taken as unity; at the school level, this is commonly approximated as a concentration of 1 M.
    
- Gases have a standard pressure of 1 bar.
    
- Pure solids and liquids are in their standard states.
    
- Temperature is specified, commonly 298 K.

Platinum:

- Conducts electricity.

3. Allows comparison of the tendencies of different electrode systems to undergo reduction.
    

## 6. Electrochemical Series

The electrochemical series is the arrangement of electrodes in order of their standard reduction potentials.

### Interpretation

- A more positive E° indicates a greater tendency to undergo reduction relative to the reference electrode.
    
- A more negative E° indicates a lower tendency to undergo reduction and, for the corresponding reverse reaction, a greater tendency to undergo oxidation.
    

### Applications

1. Predicting the anode and cathode

The electrode with the higher standard reduction potential acts as the cathode in a spontaneous galvanic cell under standard conditions.

The electrode with the lower standard reduction potential acts as the anode.

2. Calculating standard cell EMF

`E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode`

Both values must be taken as reduction potentials.

3. Predicting spontaneity

- If E°cell is positive, the cell reaction as written is spontaneous under standard conditions.
    
- If E°cell is negative, the reverse reaction is spontaneous under standard conditions.
    
- If E°cell is zero, the system is at equilibrium under standard conditions for the reaction as written.
    

4. Predicting displacement reactions

A metal with a more negative standard reduction potential can generally displace the ions of a metal with a more positive standard reduction potential from solution, provided the reaction is otherwise feasible.

Example:

`Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)`

Zinc displaces copper from copper sulphate solution.

5. Predicting oxidising and reducing strengths

- Higher reduction potential generally indicates a stronger oxidising agent in the reduced/oxidised pair comparison.
    
- Lower reduction potential generally indicates a stronger reducing agent in the corresponding reduced form.
    

## 7. EMF of an Electrochemical Cell

EMF (electromotive force) is the potential difference between the two electrodes of a cell when no current is drawn from it.

It is measured in volts (V).

### Standard EMF of a Cell

The standard EMF is calculated using standard reduction potentials.

`E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode`

For a Daniell cell:

- Standard reduction potential of Cu²⁺/Cu = +0.34 V.
    
- Standard reduction potential of Zn²⁺/Zn = −0.76 V.
    

Therefore:

`E°cell = 0.34 − (−0.76)`

`E°cell = 1.10 V`

### Important Points

- EMF is an intensive property; it does not depend on the amount of material in the cell.
    
- Multiplying a half-reaction by a coefficient does not multiply its electrode potential.
    
- Electrode potentials are not added directly as though they were ordinary extensive quantities.
    
- Under load, the terminal voltage of a real cell can differ from its EMF because of internal resistance and other losses.
    

## 8. Nernst Equation

The Nernst equation relates the electrode or cell potential to the standard potential, temperature and activities of the reacting species.

It is used when the concentrations or pressures are not at standard conditions.

### General Equation

For a cell reaction involving n electrons:

`Ecell = E°cell − (RT/nF) ln Q`

Where:

- Ecell = cell potential under the given conditions.
    
- E°cell = standard cell potential.
    
- R = gas constant, 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
    
- T = absolute temperature in kelvin.
    
- n = number of electrons transferred in the balanced cell reaction.
    
- F = Faraday constant, approximately 96485 C mol⁻¹.
    
- Q = reaction quotient.
    

### Nernst Equation at 298 K

Using base-10 logarithms:

`Ecell = E°cell − (0.0591/n) log Q`

This form is valid at approximately 298 K.

### Reaction Quotient

For a general reaction:

`aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD`

The reaction quotient is:

`Q = (aC^c × aD^d)/(aA^a × aB^b)`

Here, aA, aB, aC and aD represent the activities of the species.

For dilute solutions, activities are often approximated using relative concentrations. The activities of pure solids and pure liquids are taken as unity.

### Example: Daniell Cell

Overall reaction:

`Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)`

The reaction quotient is:

`Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺]`

Pure zinc and copper are omitted because their activities are unity.

Therefore:

`Ecell = E°cell − (0.0591/2) log([Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺])`

### Nernst Equation for a Metal-Ion Electrode

For the reduction reaction:

`Mⁿ⁺(aq) + ne⁻ → M(s)`

The equation becomes:

`E = E° + (0.0591/n) log[Mⁿ⁺]`

at 298 K, using the usual dilute-solution approximation.

### Important Observations

- If Q = 1, then Ecell = E°cell.
    
- If Q increases, the cell potential decreases for a fixed reaction and temperature.
    
- At equilibrium, Ecell = 0 and Q = K.
    
- For a spontaneous cell reaction under the specified conditions, Ecell is positive.
    

### Common Mistakes

- Using the wrong number of electrons, n.
    
- Including pure solids in Q.
    
- Reversing the reaction quotient.
    
- Using natural logarithms with the coefficient 0.0591.
    
- Using 298 K formulas at a different temperature without adjustment.
    

## 9. Relationship Between Cell Potential and Gibbs Energy

The electrical work obtained from an electrochemical cell is related to the Gibbs energy change of the cell reaction.

### Main Formula

`ΔG = −nFEcell`

Under standard conditions:

`ΔG° = −nFE°cell`

Where:

- ΔG = Gibbs energy change in J mol⁻¹ of reaction as written.
    
- n = number of electrons transferred.
    
- F = Faraday constant.
    
- Ecell = cell potential.
    

### Interpretation

Case 1: Ecell > 0

`ΔG < 0`

The cell reaction is spontaneous under the given conditions.

Case 2: Ecell < 0

`ΔG > 0`

The reaction as written is non-spontaneous under the given conditions.

Case 3: Ecell = 0

`ΔG = 0`

The system is at equilibrium.

### Relation Between Standard EMF and Equilibrium Constant

At equilibrium:

`Ecell = 0` and `Q = K`

Substituting into the Nernst equation:

`0 = E°cell − (RT/nF) ln K`

Therefore:

`E°cell = (RT/nF) ln K`

At 298 K:

`E°cell = (0.0591/n) log K`

Also:

`log K = nE°cell/0.0591`

### Important Conclusions

- A positive E°cell means K > 1.
    
- A negative E°cell means K < 1.
    
- E°cell = 0 means K = 1.
    

These conclusions apply to the balanced reaction as written, under the standard-state convention.

## 10. Conductance of Electrolytic Solutions

An electrolytic solution conducts electricity due to the movement of ions.

Examples include aqueous solutions of NaCl, HCl and CuSO₄.

### Metallic Conduction vs Electrolytic Conduction

|Metallic conduction|Electrolytic conduction|
|---|---|
|Due to movement of electrons|Due to movement of ions|
|No chemical change in the conductor itself|Electrode reactions occur during electrolysis|
|Generally decreases with increasing temperature|Generally increases with increasing temperature for typical electrolyte solutions|

### Resistance

Resistance is the opposition offered to the flow of electric current.

`R = V/I`

From the dimensions of a conductor:

`R = ρl/A`

Where:

- R = resistance.
    
- ρ = resistivity.
    
- l = length of the conductor.
    
- A = cross-sectional area.
    

SI unit: ohm (Ω).

### Conductance

Conductance (G) is the reciprocal of resistance.

`G = 1/R`

SI unit: siemens (S), equivalent to Ω⁻¹.

Greater conductance means less resistance.

### Resistivity

Resistivity (ρ) is a property that describes the resistance of a material for a given length and cross-sectional area.

`ρ = RA/l`

SI unit: Ω m.

### Conductivity

Conductivity (κ) is the reciprocal of resistivity.

`κ = 1/ρ`

Combining the equations:

`R = l/(κA)`

`G = κA/l`

SI unit: S m⁻¹.

Another commonly used unit is S cm⁻¹.

### Cell Constant

The cell constant is the ratio of the distance between the electrodes to their effective area.

`Cell constant = l/A`

Its unit is m⁻¹ or cm⁻¹, depending on the units used.

Conductivity is calculated using:

`κ = G × cell constant`

or

`κ = Cell constant/R`

### Measurement of Conductivity

A conductivity cell containing the electrolyte is connected to an instrument that measures its resistance or conductance.

The cell constant is determined using a solution of known conductivity.

The conductivity of the unknown solution is then calculated from the measured conductance and cell constant.

Important: Conductivity depends on concentration, temperature, the nature of the electrolyte and the solvent.

## 11. Molar Conductivity

Molar conductivity (Λm) is the conductance of the volume of solution containing one mole of an electrolyte, placed between electrodes separated by unit distance.

The formula is:

`Λm = κ/c`

Here, c must be expressed in mol m⁻³ when κ is in S m⁻¹.

If concentration is expressed in mol L⁻¹ and conductivity in S cm⁻¹:

`Λm = (κ × 1000)/C`

Where C is the numerical concentration in mol L⁻¹.

Common units:

- SI unit: S m² mol⁻¹.
    
- Common chemistry unit: S cm² mol⁻¹.
    

### Difference Between Conductivity and Molar Conductivity

|Conductivity (κ)|Molar conductivity (Λm)|
|---|---|
|Conductance of a unit volume of solution, expressed through its conductivity|Conductance associated with one mole of electrolyte|
|SI unit: S m⁻¹|SI unit: S m² mol⁻¹|
|Usually decreases on dilution|Increases on dilution|
|Depends on the number of ions per unit volume and their mobility|Depends on the ionic contributions per mole and their mobility|

### Effect of Dilution on Conductivity

On dilution, the number of ions per unit volume generally decreases.

Therefore, conductivity decreases.

### Effect of Dilution on Molar Conductivity

On dilution, ions experience reduced interionic interactions, and weak electrolytes undergo a greater degree of ionisation.

Therefore, molar conductivity increases.

The increase is generally:

- Relatively small for strong electrolytes.
    
- Much larger for weak electrolytes, especially near high dilution.
    

### Strong Electrolytes

Strong electrolytes are almost completely ionised in solution.

Examples: NaCl, HCl and KNO₃.

Their molar conductivity increases gradually with dilution.

For a strong electrolyte, an approximate relation is:

`Λm = Λm° − A√C`

Where:

- Λm = molar conductivity at concentration C.
    
- Λm° = limiting molar conductivity.
    
- A = a constant for a given electrolyte and temperature under the applicable model.
    

This relation is useful for sufficiently dilute strong-electrolyte solutions.

### Weak Electrolytes

Weak electrolytes are only partially ionised in solution.

Examples: CH₃COOH and NH₄OH.

Their molar conductivity increases sharply on dilution because their degree of ionisation increases significantly.

The simple linear relation used for strong electrolytes does not generally apply to weak electrolytes.

## 12. Limiting Molar Conductivity

Limiting molar conductivity (Λm°) is the molar conductivity at infinite dilution, when the concentration approaches zero.

At infinite dilution, interionic interactions become negligible.

Each ion contributes independently to the total molar conductivity.

### Strong Electrolytes

Their limiting molar conductivity can be estimated by extrapolating a suitable plot of molar conductivity against the square root of concentration towards zero concentration.

### Weak Electrolytes

Direct extrapolation is not suitable for determining their limiting molar conductivity accurately.

Instead, Kohlrausch's law is used.

## 13. Kohlrausch's Law of Independent Migration of Ions

Kohlrausch's law states that at infinite dilution, each ion contributes independently to the limiting molar conductivity of an electrolyte, regardless of the nature of the other ion present.

The limiting molar conductivity is the sum of the individual ionic contributions, multiplied by their stoichiometric coefficients.

For a general electrolyte producing ν₊ cations and ν₋ anions:

`Λm° = ν₊λ₊° + ν₋λ₋°`

Where:

- λ₊° = limiting ionic conductivity of the cation.
    
- λ₋° = limiting ionic conductivity of the anion.
    

### Example: Sodium Chloride

`Λm°(NaCl) = λ°Na⁺ + λ°Cl⁻`

### Example: Calcium Chloride

`Λm°(CaCl₂) = λ°Ca²⁺ + 2λ°Cl⁻`

The coefficient 2 is used because one formula unit of CaCl₂ produces two chloride ions.

### Applications of Kohlrausch's Law

1. Determination of limiting molar conductivity of weak electrolytes

For acetic acid:

`Λm°(CH₃COOH) = Λm°(HCl) + Λm°(CH₃COONa) − Λm°(NaCl)`

This combination cancels the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ contributions, leaving the contributions of H⁺ and CH₃COO⁻.

2. Determination of degree of dissociation

For a weak electrolyte:

`α = Λm/Λm°`

Where α is the degree of dissociation.

This relationship assumes the electrolyte's conductivity is due to the ions produced by its dissociation.

3. Determination of dissociation constant

For a weak 1:1 electrolyte of initial concentration C:

`Ka = Cα²/(1 − α)`

Substituting α = Λm/Λm°:

`Ka = CΛm²/[Λm°(Λm° − Λm)]`

This expression applies to a weak acid under the usual ideal dilute-solution approximation.

### Important Exam Point

Kohlrausch's law is especially useful for calculating the limiting molar conductivity of weak electrolytes because their limiting values cannot be obtained accurately through ordinary extrapolation.

## 14. Electrolysis

Electrolysis is the process in which electrical energy is used to bring about a non-spontaneous chemical reaction.

It takes place in an electrolytic cell.

### Working of an Electrolytic Cell

- The external source supplies electrical energy.
    
- Cations move towards the cathode and gain electrons.
    
- Anions move towards the anode and lose electrons.
    
- Oxidation takes place at the anode.
    
- Reduction takes place at the cathode.
    

### Discharge of Ions

During electrolysis, ions may gain or lose electrons at the electrodes to form products.

The products depend on factors such as:

- Nature of the electrolyte.
    
- Nature of the electrodes.
    
- Concentration of the ions.
    
- Electrode potentials and the conditions of electrolysis.
    

### Electrolysis of Molten NaCl

Molten sodium chloride contains Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.

At the cathode:

`Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na(l)`

At the anode:

`2Cl⁻ → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻`

Overall reaction:

`2NaCl(l) → 2Na(l) + Cl₂(g)`

Since the salt is molten, water is absent and sodium metal can be produced at the cathode.

### Electrolysis of Aqueous NaCl

In concentrated aqueous sodium chloride with suitable inert electrodes:

At the cathode:

`2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂(g) + 2OH⁻`

At the anode:

`2Cl⁻ → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻`

The overall reaction is:

`2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g) + Cl₂(g)`

The exact products of aqueous electrolysis can depend on the electrode material and experimental conditions.

## 15. Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis

Faraday's laws relate the amount of chemical change at an electrode to the electric charge passed through the electrolyte.

### First Law of Electrolysis

The mass of a substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity passed through the electrolyte, provided the relevant conditions remain fixed.

`m ∝ Q`

Since:

`Q = It`

Therefore:

`m = ZIt`

Where:

- m = mass deposited or liberated.
    
- Q = electric charge.
    
- I = current in amperes.
    
- t = time in seconds.
    
- Z = electrochemical equivalent.
    

### Electrochemical Equivalent

Electrochemical equivalent is the mass of a substance deposited or liberated by passing one coulomb of charge.

SI unit: kg C⁻¹.

### Second Law of Electrolysis

When the same quantity of electricity passes through different electrolytes, the masses of substances deposited or liberated are proportional to their chemical equivalent masses.

`m₁/m₂ = E₁/E₂`

Where E₁ and E₂ represent the equivalent masses of the substances.

For a species requiring n electrons per ion:

`Equivalent mass = Molar mass/n`

### Faraday Constant

One mole of electrons carries approximately 96485 coulombs of charge.

`F ≈ 96485 C mol⁻¹`

### Main Electrolysis Formula

Using the molar mass M and number of electrons n:

`m = MIt/(nF)`

This is one of the most important formulas for electrolysis numericals.

### Number of Moles Deposited

`Moles = It/(nF)`

### Charge Passed

`Q = It`

Where current is in amperes and time is in seconds.

### Useful Conversions

- 1 A = 1 C s⁻¹.
    
- 1 hour = 3600 seconds.
    
- 1 Faraday corresponds to approximately 96485 C per mole of electrons.
    

### Example

A current of 2 A is passed for 30 minutes.

Charge passed:

`Q = It`

`Q = 2 × 30 × 60`

`Q = 3600 C`

Moles of electrons transferred:

`Moles of electrons = Q/F`

`= 3600/96485`

`≈ 0.0373 mol`

To calculate the mass of a deposited metal, divide the number of moles of electrons by the number of electrons required per metal ion, then multiply by the metal's molar mass.

### Common Mistakes

- Using minutes instead of seconds.
    
- Forgetting to divide by n.
    
- Using molar mass instead of equivalent mass in a formula that requires equivalent mass.
    
- Forgetting that the same charge can produce different masses of different substances.
    

## 16. Batteries

A battery is a device consisting of one or more electrochemical cells that supplies electrical energy through chemical reactions.

Batteries are classified into primary and secondary batteries.

### Primary Batteries

Primary batteries cannot normally be recharged by reversing their chemical reactions under ordinary operating conditions.

Once the reactants are consumed, the battery is discarded.

Examples:

- Dry cell.
    
- Mercury cell.
    

### Secondary Batteries

Secondary batteries can be recharged by passing an external current through them, reversing the cell reaction to a useful extent.

Examples:

- Lead storage battery.
    
- Lithium-ion battery.
    

### Difference Between Primary and Secondary Batteries

|Primary battery|Secondary battery|
|---|---|
|Generally non-rechargeable|Rechargeable|
|Cell reaction is not practically reversible under ordinary use|Cell reaction can be reversed during charging|
|Used until its useful reactants are consumed|Can be used over multiple charge-discharge cycles|
|Example: dry cell|Example: lead storage battery|

## 17. Dry Cell

A dry cell is a primary battery commonly used in small electrical devices.

It is also known as a Leclanché cell.

### Construction

The main components are:

- Zinc container, which acts as the anode.
    
- Carbon rod, which acts as the current collector for the cathode mixture.
    
- Manganese dioxide and carbon powder surrounding the carbon rod.
    
- Moist paste containing ammonium chloride and zinc chloride as the electrolyte.
    

### Working

The zinc container undergoes oxidation, releasing electrons into the external circuit.

At the cathode, manganese dioxide participates in the reduction process.

The dry cell produces a voltage of approximately 1.5 V when fresh, although the actual voltage varies with the cell's condition and load.

### Important Points

- It is a primary battery.
    
- It is not designed for ordinary recharging.
    
- The zinc container is consumed during discharge.
    

## 18. Mercury Cell

A mercury cell is a primary battery that was traditionally used in small devices such as watches and hearing aids.

### Cell Components

- Anode: Zinc amalgam.
    
- Cathode: Mercury(II) oxide mixed with carbon.
    
- Electrolyte: A paste containing KOH or NaOH.
    

### Electrode Reactions

At the anode:

`Zn(Hg) + 2OH⁻ → ZnO(s) + H₂O + 2e⁻`

At the cathode:

`HgO(s) + H₂O + 2e⁻ → Hg(l) + 2OH⁻`

Overall reaction:

`Zn(Hg) + HgO(s) → ZnO(s) + Hg(l)`

### Important Characteristics

- It produces a nearly constant potential during much of its useful discharge.
    
- The overall reaction does not involve a net change in the amount of hydroxide ions.
    
- Mercury is toxic, so mercury-containing batteries require appropriate disposal and have largely been replaced by alternatives in many applications.
    

## 19. Lead Storage Battery

A lead storage battery is a secondary battery that can be recharged.

It is commonly used in automobiles and backup power systems.

### Construction

The main components are:

- Anode during discharge: Lead (Pb).
    
- Cathode during discharge: Lead dioxide (PbO₂).
    
- Electrolyte: Aqueous sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄).
    

### Reactions During Discharge

At the anode:

`Pb(s) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → PbSO₄(s) + 2e⁻`

At the cathode:

`PbO₂(s) + 4H⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + 2e⁻ → PbSO₄(s) + 2H₂O(l)`

Overall reaction:

`Pb(s) + PbO₂(s) + 2H₂SO₄(aq) → 2PbSO₄(s) + 2H₂O(l)`

### During Charging

An external power source drives the reactions in the reverse direction.

- Lead sulphate at the negative plate is converted back to lead.
    
- Lead sulphate at the positive plate is converted back to lead dioxide.
    
- Sulphuric acid concentration increases as the battery returns towards its charged state.
    

### Important Points

- It is a secondary battery.
    
- Both electrodes form lead sulphate during discharge.
    
- The concentration of sulphuric acid decreases during discharge.
    
- The nominal voltage of a single lead-acid cell is approximately 2 V.
    
- A typical automobile battery contains six cells connected in series, giving a nominal voltage of approximately 12 V.
    

## 20. Fuel Cells

A fuel cell is an electrochemical cell that converts the chemical energy of a continuously supplied fuel and oxidant directly into electrical energy.

Unlike a conventional battery, it can continue operating as long as the reactants are supplied and the products are removed.

### Hydrogen-Oxygen Fuel Cell

In a hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell, hydrogen acts as the fuel and oxygen acts as the oxidant.

In the alkaline-cell representation commonly used in school chemistry:

At the anode:

`2H₂(g) + 4OH⁻(aq) → 4H₂O(l) + 4e⁻`

At the cathode:

`O₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻(aq)`

Overall reaction:

`2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)`

### Advantages

- High efficiency compared with many conventional energy-conversion systems.
    
- Continuous electricity generation when fuel and oxidant are supplied.
    
- Water is the overall reaction product for the hydrogen-oxygen system.
    
- Useful in spacecraft and other specialised power systems.
    

### Limitations

- Hydrogen production, storage and transportation can be challenging.
    
- Fuel-cell systems can be expensive.
    
- The overall environmental impact depends on how the fuel is produced and how the system is operated.
    

### Fuel Cell vs Battery

|Fuel cell|Battery|
|---|---|
|Reactants are supplied continuously from outside|Reactants are generally stored within the cell|
|Can operate while reactants are supplied|Requires recharging or replacement after discharge|
|Converts fuel energy directly into electrical energy|Converts stored chemical energy into electrical energy|

## 21. Corrosion

Corrosion is the gradual deterioration of a metal due to chemical or electrochemical reactions with its environment.

Examples include rusting of iron, tarnishing of silver and the formation of a green layer on copper.

### Rusting of Iron

Rusting is an electrochemical process that occurs in the presence of moisture and oxygen.

Different regions on the iron surface behave as tiny anodic and cathodic areas.

### Anodic Reaction

Iron undergoes oxidation:

`Fe(s) → Fe²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻`

### Cathodic Reaction

In neutral or alkaline, aerated water, oxygen is reduced:

`O₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻(aq)`

### Formation of Rust

The ferrous ions react with hydroxide ions to form iron(II) hydroxide:

`Fe²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₂(s)`

Further oxidation produces iron(III) hydroxide and related hydrated iron(III) oxide products.

Rust is commonly represented as hydrated iron(III) oxide:

`Fe₂O₃ · xH₂O`

The value of x varies because rust is not a single compound of fixed composition.

### Factors Affecting Corrosion

- Presence of moisture.
    
- Presence of oxygen.
    
- Dissolved salts, which increase the conductivity of the water film.
    
- Acidic conditions in many environments.
    
- Contact between dissimilar metals under suitable conditions.
    

### Prevention of Corrosion

1. Painting or coating

Creates a barrier between the metal and the environment.

2. Oiling and greasing

Reduces contact with moisture and oxygen.

3. Galvanisation

Iron or steel is coated with zinc. Zinc provides a protective barrier and can also protect the iron sacrificially if the coating is damaged.

4. Alloying

Certain alloys, such as stainless steel, have improved corrosion resistance because they form protective surface layers.

5. Cathodic protection

The metal to be protected is made the cathode by connecting it to a suitable sacrificial anode or an external electrical source.

### Why Does Zinc Protect Iron?

Zinc has a more negative standard reduction potential than iron.

When zinc and iron are electrically connected under suitable conditions, zinc can oxidise preferentially, protecting the iron from oxidation.

This is called sacrificial protection.

## 23. Common Exam Mistakes

- Cell EMF: Subtract the anode's reduction potential from the cathode's reduction potential. Do not reverse the subtraction.
    
- Electrode potentials: Multiplying a half-reaction by a number does not multiply its electrode potential.
    
- Nernst equation: Write the balanced overall reaction first, then construct Q.
    
- Reaction quotient: Exclude pure solids and pure liquids.
    
- Temperature: Use the 0.0591 coefficient only at approximately 298 K.
    
- Gibbs energy: A positive cell potential corresponds to a negative Gibbs energy change for the same reaction.
    
- Conductivity: Usually decreases on dilution.
    
- Molar conductivity: Increases on dilution.
    
- Weak electrolytes: Their molar conductivity increases sharply on dilution due to increased ionisation.
    
- Kohlrausch's law: Multiply each ionic contribution by its stoichiometric coefficient.
    
- Electrolysis: Convert time into seconds before calculating charge.
    
- Faraday's law: Divide by n when calculating the moles of a species from the moles of electrons.
    
- Electrode signs: The anode is negative in a galvanic cell but positive in an electrolytic cell. Oxidation always occurs at the anode.
