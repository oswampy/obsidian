# Electrochemistry 


## 1. Electrochemistry — Introduction

Electrochemistry is the branch of chemistry that deals with the relationship between chemical energy and electrical energy.

It mainly involves two types of processes:

- Chemical energy to electrical energy: Occurs in galvanic or voltaic cells.
    
- Electrical energy to chemical energy: Occurs in electrolytic cells.
    

### Redox Reactions

Electrochemical processes are based on oxidation-reduction (redox) reactions.

- Oxidation: Loss of electrons.
    
- Reduction: Gain of electrons.
    
- Oxidising agent: Accepts electrons and gets reduced.
    
- Reducing agent: Donates electrons and gets oxidised.
    

Example:

`Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)`

- Zinc loses electrons and is oxidised.
    
- Copper ions gain electrons and are reduced.
    
- Zinc acts as the reducing agent.
    
- Copper ions act as the oxidising agent.
    

Half-reactions:

- Oxidation: `Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻`
    
- Reduction: `Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)`
    

## 2. Electrochemical Cells

An electrochemical cell is a device in which a redox reaction is used to convert chemical energy into electrical energy or electrical energy into chemical energy.

There are two main types.

### Galvanic or Voltaic Cell

A galvanic cell converts the chemical energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy.

Characteristics:

- The redox reaction is spontaneous.
    
- Oxidation occurs at the anode.
    
- Reduction occurs at the cathode.
    
- Electrons flow through the external circuit from anode to cathode.
    
- The anode is negative and the cathode is positive.
    

### Electrolytic Cell

An electrolytic cell uses electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous chemical reaction.

Characteristics:

- The reaction is non-spontaneous under the applied conditions.
    
- Oxidation occurs at the anode.
    
- Reduction occurs at the cathode.
    
- The anode is positive and the cathode is negative.
    

### Difference Between Galvanic and Electrolytic Cells

|Property|Galvanic Cell|Electrolytic Cell|
|---|---|---|
|Energy conversion|Chemical to electrical|Electrical to chemical|
|Reaction|Spontaneous|Non-spontaneous|
|Anode|Negative|Positive|
|Cathode|Positive|Negative|
|Oxidation|At anode|At anode|
|Reduction|At cathode|At cathode|
|Electron flow externally|Anode to cathode|Driven by the external power source|

Important: Oxidation always occurs at the anode and reduction always occurs at the cathode. The signs of the electrodes depend on the type of cell.

## 3. Daniell Cell

A Daniell cell is a galvanic cell in which zinc and copper electrodes are immersed in solutions containing their respective ions.

It converts the chemical energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy.

### Construction

The Daniell cell consists of:

- A zinc rod dipped in zinc sulphate solution.
    
- A copper rod dipped in copper sulphate solution.
    
- A salt bridge connecting the two solutions.
    
- An external wire connecting the two electrodes.
    

### Working

At the zinc electrode (anode):

Zinc undergoes oxidation and releases electrons.

`Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻`

- The zinc electrode gradually loses mass.
    
- The concentration of zinc ions in the solution increases.
    

At the copper electrode (cathode):

Copper ions undergo reduction by accepting electrons.

`Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)`

- Copper gets deposited on the copper electrode.
    
- The concentration of copper ions in the solution decreases.
    

### Overall Cell Reaction

`Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)`

### Cell Representation

The Daniell cell is represented as:

`Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)`

Meaning of the symbols:

- `|` represents a phase boundary.
    
- `||` represents the salt bridge.
    
- The anode is written on the left.
    
- The cathode is written on the right.
    

### Functions of a Salt Bridge

A salt bridge is a tube containing an electrolyte, usually an inert salt such as KCl or KNO₃, in a suitable medium.

Its functions are:

1. Completes the electrical circuit by allowing ions to migrate.
    
2. Maintains electrical neutrality in both half-cells.
    
3. Prevents the direct mixing of the two electrolyte solutions.
    
4. Minimises liquid junction potential.
    

Ion movement in the Daniell cell:

- Anions from the salt bridge migrate towards the anode compartment, where positive ions are being produced.
    
- Cations from the salt bridge migrate towards the cathode compartment, where positive ions are being consumed.
    

Important: Electrons travel through the external wire, whereas ions travel through the electrolyte and salt bridge.

## 4. Electrode Potential

Electrode potential is the potential difference developed between an electrode and the electrolyte in which it is immersed due to the tendency of the electrode species to undergo oxidation or reduction.

When a metal is dipped into a solution containing its ions, two competing processes may occur:

- Metal atoms may lose electrons and enter the solution as positive ions.
    
- Positive ions from the solution may gain electrons and deposit as metal atoms.
    

An equilibrium may develop between these processes.

### Development of Electrode Potential

For a metal M:

`M(s) ⇌ Mⁿ⁺(aq) + ne⁻`

The tendency of an electrode to undergo reduction is expressed through its reduction potential.

A higher reduction potential indicates a greater tendency to undergo reduction relative to the reference electrode under the specified conditions.

### Oxidation Potential and Reduction Potential

- Reduction potential: Tendency to gain electrons.
    
- Oxidation potential: Tendency to lose electrons.
    

For the same electrode reaction under identical conditions:

`E°oxidation = −E°reduction`

Important: Electrode potential cannot be measured independently as an absolute value. It is measured relative to a reference electrode.

## 5. Standard Electrode Potential

Standard electrode potential, represented by E°, is the electrode potential measured under standard conditions relative to the standard hydrogen electrode.

### Standard Conditions

- Solute activities are taken as unity; at the school level, this is commonly approximated as a concentration of 1 M.
    
- Gases have a standard pressure of 1 bar.
    
- Pure solids and liquids are in their standard states.
    
- Temperature is specified, commonly 298 K.
    

### Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

### Why Is Platinum Used?

Platinum:

- Conducts electricity.
    
- Is chemically inert under the relevant conditions.
    
- Provides a surface for the hydrogen electrode reaction.
    
- Does not take part in the overall reaction.
    

### Importance of SHE

1. Provides a reference for measuring electrode potentials.
    
2. Helps determine the standard reduction potentials of other electrodes.
    
3. Allows comparison of the tendencies of different electrode systems to undergo reduction.
    

## 6. Electrochemical Series

The electrochemical series is the arrangement of electrodes in order of their standard reduction potentials.

### Interpretation

- A more positive E° indicates a greater tendency to undergo reduction relative to the reference electrode.
    
- A more negative E° indicates a lower tendency to undergo reduction and, for the corresponding reverse reaction, a greater tendency to undergo oxidation.
    

### Applications

1. Predicting the anode and cathode

The electrode with the higher standard reduction potential acts as the cathode in a spontaneous galvanic cell under standard conditions.

The electrode with the lower standard reduction potential acts as the anode.

2. Calculating standard cell EMF

`E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode`

Both values must be taken as reduction potentials.

3. Predicting spontaneity

- If E°cell is positive, the cell reaction as written is spontaneous under standard conditions.
    
- If E°cell is negative, the reverse reaction is spontaneous under standard conditions.
    
- If E°cell is zero, the system is at equilibrium under standard conditions for the reaction as written.
    

4. Predicting displacement reactions

A metal with a more negative standard reduction potential can generally displace the ions of a metal with a more positive standard reduction potential from solution, provided the reaction is otherwise feasible.

Example:

`Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)`

Zinc displaces copper from copper sulphate solution.

5. Predicting oxidising and reducing strengths

- Higher reduction potential generally indicates a stronger oxidising agent in the reduced/oxidised pair comparison.
    
- Lower reduction potential generally indicates a stronger reducing agent in the corresponding reduced form.
    

## 7. EMF of an Electrochemical Cell

EMF (electromotive force) is the potential difference between the two electrodes of a cell when no current is drawn from it.

It is measured in volts (V).

### Standard EMF of a Cell

The standard EMF is calculated using standard reduction potentials.

`E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode`

For a Daniell cell:

- Standard reduction potential of Cu²⁺/Cu = +0.34 V.
    
- Standard reduction potential of Zn²⁺/Zn = −0.76 V.
    

Therefore:

`E°cell = 0.34 − (−0.76)`

`E°cell = 1.10 V`

### Important Points

- EMF is an intensive property; it does not depend on the amount of material in the cell.
    
- Multiplying a half-reaction by a coefficient does not multiply its electrode potential.
    
- Electrode potentials are not added directly as though they were ordinary extensive quantities.
    
- Under load, the terminal voltage of a real cell can differ from its EMF because of internal resistance and other losses.
    

## 8. Nernst Equation

The Nernst equation relates the electrode or cell potential to the standard potential, temperature and activities of the reacting species.

It is used when the concentrations or pressures are not at standard conditions.

### General Equation

For a cell reaction involving n electrons:

`Ecell = E°cell − (RT/nF) ln Q`

Where
	- Ecell = cell potential under the given conditions.
	    
	- E°cell = standard cell potential.
	    
	- R = gas constant, 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
	    
	- T = absolute temperature in kelvin.
	    
	- n = number of electrons transferred in the balanced cell reaction.
	    
	- F = Faraday constant, approximately 96485 C mol⁻¹.
	    
	- Q = reaction quotient.
	    

### Nernst Equation at 298 K

Using base-10 logarithms:

`Ecell = E°cell − (0.0591/n) log Q`

This form is valid at approximately 298 K.

### Reaction Quotient

For a general reaction:

`aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD`

The reaction quotient is:

`Q = (aC^c × aD^d)/(aA^a × aB^b)`

Here, aA, aB, aC and aD represent the activities of the species.

For dilute solutions, activities are often approximated using relative concentrations. The activities of pure solids and pure liquids are taken as unity.

### Example: Daniell Cell

Overall reaction:

`Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)`

The reaction quotient is:

`Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺]`

Pure zinc and copper are omitted because their activities are unity.

Therefore:

`Ecell = E°cell − (0.0591/2) log([Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺])`

### Nernst Equation for a Metal-Ion Electrode

For the reduction reaction:

`Mⁿ⁺(aq) + ne⁻ → M(s)`

The equation becomes:

`E = E° + (0.0591/n) log[Mⁿ⁺]`

at 298 K, using the usual dilute-solution approximation.

### Important Observations

- If Q = 1, then Ecell = E°cell.
    
- If Q increases, the cell potential decreases for a fixed reaction and temperature.
    
- At equilibrium, Ecell = 0 and Q = K.
    
- For a spontaneous cell reaction under the specified conditions, Ecell is positive.
    


## 9. Relationship Between Cell Potential and Gibbs Energy

The electrical work obtained from an electrochemical cell is related to the Gibbs energy change of the cell reaction.

### Main Formula

`ΔG = −nFEcell`

Under standard conditions:

`ΔG° = −nFE°cell`

Where:

- ΔG = Gibbs energy change in J mol⁻¹ of reaction as written.
    
- n = number of electrons transferred.
    
- F = Faraday constant.
    
- Ecell = cell potential.
    

### Interpretation

Case 1: Ecell > 0

`ΔG < 0`

The cell reaction is spontaneous under the given conditions.

Case 2: Ecell < 0

`ΔG > 0`

The reaction as written is non-spontaneous under the given conditions.

Case 3: Ecell = 0

`ΔG = 0`

The system is at equilibrium.

### Relation Between Standard EMF and Equilibrium Constant

At equilibrium:

`Ecell = 0` and `Q = K`

Substituting into the Nernst equation:

`0 = E°cell − (RT/nF) ln K`

Therefore:

`E°cell = (RT/nF) ln K`

At 298 K:

`E°cell = (0.0591/n) log K`

Also:

`log K = nE°cell/0.0591`

### Important Conclusions

- A positive E°cell means K > 1.
    
- A negative E°cell means K < 1.
    
- E°cell = 0 means K = 1.
    

These conclusions apply to the balanced reaction as written, under the standard-state convention.

## 10. Conductance of Electrolytic Solutions

An electrolytic solution conducts electricity due to the movement of ions.

Examples include aqueous solutions of NaCl, HCl and CuSO₄.

### Metallic Conduction vs Electrolytic Conduction

|Metallic conduction|Electrolytic conduction|
|---|---|
|Due to movement of electrons|Due to movement of ions|
|No chemical change in the conductor itself|Electrode reactions occur during electrolysis|
|Generally decreases with increasing temperature|Generally increases with increasing temperature for typical electrolyte solutions|

### Resistance

Resistance is the opposition offered to the flow of electric current.

`R = V/I`

From the dimensions of a conductor:

`R = ρl/A`

Where:

- R = resistance.
    
- ρ = resistivity.
    
- l = length of the conductor.
    
- A = cross-sectional area.
    

SI unit: ohm (Ω).

### Conductance

Conductance (G) is the reciprocal of resistance.

`G = 1/R`

SI unit: siemens (S), equivalent to Ω⁻¹.

Greater conductance means less resistance.

### Resistivity

Resistivity (ρ) is a property that describes the resistance of a material for a given length and cross-sectional area.

`ρ = RA/l`

SI unit: Ω m.

### Conductivity

Conductivity (κ) is the reciprocal of resistivity.

`κ = 1/ρ`

Combining the equations:

`R = l/(κA)`

`G = κA/l`

SI unit: S m⁻¹.

Another commonly used unit is S cm⁻¹.

### Cell Constant

The cell constant is the ratio of the distance between the electrodes to their effective area.

`Cell constant = l/A`

Its unit is m⁻¹ or cm⁻¹, depending on the units used.

Conductivity is calculated using:

`κ = G × cell constant`

or

`κ = Cell constant/R`

### Measurement of Conductivity

A conductivity cell containing the electrolyte is connected to an instrument that measures its resistance or conductance.

The cell constant is determined using a solution of known conductivity.

The conductivity of the unknown solution is then calculated from the measured conductance and cell constant.

Important: Conductivity depends on concentration, temperature, the nature of the electrolyte and the solvent.
