## 5. The Lanthanoids

### 5.1 Introduction

The lanthanoids are the elements in which the 4f orbitals are progressively filled.

They are commonly discussed as the series from cerium to lutetium, while lanthanum is closely associated with the series.

### 5.2 Electronic Configuration

The general electronic configuration is:

$[Xe]4f^{1-14}5d^{0-1}6s^2$

The configurations of some lanthanoids contain exceptions because the 4f and 5d orbitals have similar energies.

### 5.3 Oxidation States

The most common oxidation state of lanthanoids is $+3$.

This occurs because they generally lose two 6s electrons and one additional electron.

Some lanthanoids also show $+2$ and $+4$ oxidation states.

Examples:

- $Ce^{4+}$
    
- $Eu^{2+}$
    
- $Yb^{2+}$
    
- $Tb^{4+}$
    

These additional oxidation states may be favoured when they produce relatively stable empty, half-filled or filled 4f subshells.

### 5.4 Chemical Reactivity

Lanthanoids are electropositive metals and are generally reactive.
reactions similar to aluminium
They react with:

- Oxygen to form oxides.
    
- Water to form hydroxides and hydrogen.
    
- Halogens to form halides.
    
- Dilute acids to release hydrogen.
    

General reactions:

$4Ln+3O_2\rightarrow2Ln_2O_3$

$2Ln+6H_2O\rightarrow2Ln(OH)_3+3H_2$

$2Ln+3Cl_2\rightarrow2LnCl_3$

Here, $Ln$ represents a lanthanoid metal; the exact products can vary for particular elements and conditions.

Notes across the series (in ground state):
- d1 is present in La,Ce,Gd,Lu
- f2,f8 not present
- f7 in Eu,Gd
- f14 in Yb,Lu

### 5.5 Lanthanoid Contraction

**Lanthanoid contraction** is the gradual decrease in the atomic and ionic radii of lanthanoids with increasing atomic number.

#### Cause

- Nuclear charge increases across the series.
    
- Electrons are added to the 4f subshell.
    
- 4f electrons shield the nuclear charge poorly.
    
- Effective nuclear attraction therefore increases.
    

#### Consequences

**1. Similarity of lanthanoids**

The lanthanoids have similar ionic radii and predominantly show the $+3$ oxidation state. Therefore, separating them from one another is difficult.

**2. Similarity between 4d and 5d elements**

Elements such as Zr and Hf have nearly equal atomic radii.

As a result, they show very similar chemical properties.

**3. Decrease in basicity of hydroxides**

The basicity of lanthanoid hydroxides generally decreases from $La(OH)_3$ to $Lu(OH)_3$.

This is because ionic size decreases and the polarising power of the metal ion increases.

## 6. The Actinoids

### 6.1 Introduction

The actinoids are the elements in which the 5f orbitals are progressively filled.

The series extends from actinium to lawrencium.

All actinoids are radioactive.

### 6.2 Electronic Configuration

The general electronic configuration is:

$[Rn]5f^{0-14}6d^{0-1}7s^2$

The exact configuration varies because the energies of the 5f, 6d and 7s orbitals are close.

### 6.3 Oxidation States

Actinoids show a wider range of oxidation states than lanthanoids.

This is because the energies of the 5f, 6d and 7s electrons are relatively close.

Common oxidation states include $+3$, $+4$, $+5$ and $+6$.

Some actinoids can also show higher oxidation states.

Examples:

- U: $+3,+4,+5,+6$
    
- Np: several oxidation states, including $+3$ to $+7$
    
- Pu: several oxidation states, including $+3$ to $+7$
    

The stability of each oxidation state depends on the element and the chemical environment.

### 6.4 Chemical Reactivity

Actinoids are electropositive and generally reactive metals.

They can react with oxygen, halogens, acids and other substances. Their chemical behaviour varies with oxidation state.

### 6.5 Actinoid Contraction

**Actinoid contraction** is the gradual decrease in the atomic and ionic radii of actinoids with increasing atomic number.

#### Cause

The nuclear charge increases, but 5f electrons shield the nucleus poorly.

Therefore, the effective nuclear attraction increases across the series.

### 6.6 Comparison of Lanthanoids and Actinoids

|Property|Lanthanoids|Actinoids|
|---|---|---|
|Orbital being filled|4f|5f|
|General configuration|$[Xe]4f^{1-14}5d^{0-1}6s^2$|$[Rn]5f^{0-14}6d^{0-1}7s^2$|
|Common oxidation state|+3|+3, with several others|
|Range of oxidation states|Usually limited|Generally wider|
|Radioactivity|Most are non-radioactive; promethium is radioactive|All are radioactive|
|Contraction|Lanthanoid contraction|Actinoid contraction|
|Complex formation|Occurs|More pronounced tendency in many cases|
|Shielding|4f electrons shield poorly|5f electrons shield poorly|

## 7. Applications of d- and f-Block Elements

### 7.1 Applications of Transition Metals

- Iron is used in construction and steel production.
    
- Copper is used in electrical wiring.
    
- Chromium is used in stainless steel and protective coatings.
    
- Nickel is used in alloys and as a catalyst.
    
- Titanium is used in strong, lightweight alloys.
    
- Manganese compounds are used as oxidising agents.
    
- Vanadium compounds are used as catalysts.
    

### 7.2 Applications of Lanthanoids

- Certain lanthanoid compounds are used in phosphors and display technologies.
    
- Cerium compounds are used in glass polishing.
    
- Lanthanoid-containing alloys are used in specialised materials.
    
- Some lanthanoids are used in permanent magnets.
    

### 7.3 Applications of Actinoids

- Uranium is used as fuel in nuclear reactors.
    
- Plutonium is used in certain nuclear-energy applications.
    
- Some actinoids are used in scientific research and specialised nuclear applications.
    

## 8. Important Reasons for CBSE Examinations

### 8.1 Why Do Transition Elements Show Variable Oxidation States?

The ns and $(n-1)d$ electrons have comparable energies, so different numbers of these electrons can participate in bonding.

### 8.2 Why Are Transition Metals Good Catalysts?

They show variable oxidation states, form intermediate compounds and can adsorb reactants on their surfaces.

### 8.3 Why Do Transition Metals Form Coloured Ions?

Partially filled d-orbitals can undergo d–d transitions by absorbing visible light.

### 8.4 Why Do Transition Metals Form Complexes?

Their ions generally have small sizes, high charge densities and the ability to accept electron pairs from ligands.

### 8.5 Why Do Transition Metals Have High Melting Points?

They generally have strong metallic bonding due to the participation of ns and d electrons.

### 8.6 Why Are $Zn^{2+}$ and $Sc^{3+}$ Colourless?

$Zn^{2+}$ has a $3d^{10}$ configuration, while $Sc^{3+}$ has a $3d^0$ configuration. Neither has the partially filled d-orbitals needed for ordinary d–d transitions.

### 8.7 Why Is $Mn^{2+}$ Particularly Stable?

$Mn^{2+}$ has a half-filled $3d^5$ configuration, which provides extra stability.

### 8.8 Why Is $Fe^{3+}$ Relatively Stable?

$Fe^{3+}$ has a half-filled $3d^5$ configuration, which is relatively stable.

### 8.9 Why Is the Highest Oxidation State Often Found Near the Middle of the Series?

Near the middle, more d-electrons are available for bonding, allowing a greater number of electrons to participate in oxidation.

### 8.10 Why Are Lanthanoids Difficult to Separate?

They have very similar ionic radii and commonly show the same $+3$ oxidation state.

### 8.11 Why Do Zr and Hf Have Similar Properties?

Lanthanoid contraction makes their atomic and ionic radii very similar, leading to similar chemical properties.

### 8.12 Why Do Actinoids Show More Oxidation States?

The 5f, 6d and 7s orbitals have comparable energies, allowing different numbers of electrons to participate in bonding.
