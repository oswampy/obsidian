
## 1. Introduction to d- and f-Block Elements

### What Are d-Block Elements?

**d-block elements** are the elements in which the last electron enters the penultimate d-subshell.

- They are located in Groups 3–12 of the periodic table.
    
- They lie between the s-block and p-block elements.
    
- They are also called transition elements, although not every d-block element is a transition element.
    
- They generally show metallic properties, variable oxidation states and catalytic activity.
    

### What Are f-Block Elements?

**f-block elements** are the elements in which the last electron enters the antepenultimate f-subshell.

- They are placed separately at the bottom of the periodic table.
    
- They consist of two series:
    
    - Lanthanoids: filling of 4f orbitals.
        
    - Actinoids: filling of 5f orbitals.
        
- They are also called inner transition elements.
    

### d-Block Elements vs Transition Elements

**Transition elements** are elements whose atoms have an incomplete d-subshell or whose ions have an incomplete d-subshell.

- Zn, Cd and Hg are d-block elements but are generally not considered transition elements because their atoms and common $+2$ ions have completely filled d-orbitals.
    
- Cu is a transition element because $Cu^{2+}$ has an incomplete d-subshell.
    

## 2. Position in the Periodic Table

### Different Transition Series

1. First transition series: Sc to Zn (3d series).
    
2. Second transition series: Y to Cd (4d series).
    
3. Third transition series: La, Hf to Hg (5d series, with La often placed in the lanthanoid series).
    
4. Fourth transition series: Ac and the 6d elements (incomplete series).
    

### General Electronic Configuration

The general electronic configuration of d-block elements is:

$(n-1)d^{1-10}ns^{0-2}$

The exact configuration depends on the element.

### Why Are Cr and Cu Exceptions?

The expected configurations would be $3d^4 4s^2$ for Cr and $3d^9 4s^2$ for Cu.

However:

- Cr has the configuration $3d^5 4s^1$.
    
- Cu has the configuration $3d^{10}4s^1$.
    

**Reason:** Half-filled and completely filled d-subshells have extra stability due to symmetrical electron distribution and exchange energy.

### Electronic Configuration of Ions

When transition metals form cations, electrons are removed from the ns orbital before the $(n-1)d$ orbital.

Examples:

- Fe: $[Ar]3d^6 4s^2$
    
- $Fe^{2+}$: $[Ar]3d^6$
    
- $Fe^{3+}$: $[Ar]3d^5$
    
- Cu: $[Ar]3d^{10}4s^1$
    
- $Cu^+$: $[Ar]3d^{10}$
    
- $Cu^{2+}$: $[Ar]3d^9$
    

## 3. General Properties of Transition Elements

### 3.1 Metallic Character

Transition elements are metals because they generally have:

- High tensile strength.
    
- High electrical and thermal conductivity.
    
- Metallic lustre.
    
- Malleability and ductility.
    

They usually have high melting and boiling points because of strong metallic bonding.

### 3.2 Atomic and Ionic Radii

Across a transition series, atomic radii generally decrease gradually with increasing atomic number.

**Reason:** Nuclear charge increases, while the added d-electrons provide only partial shielding.

The decrease is relatively small because the added electrons also increase electron–electron repulsion.

- Atomic radii decrease slightly from Sc to Cr.
    
- The change becomes small towards the end of the series.
    
- Elements of the second and third transition series often have similar radii because of lanthanoid contraction.

### 3.4 Oxidation States

Transition elements show variable oxidation states because the energies of the ns and $(n-1)d$ electrons are comparable.

Both types of electrons can participate in bond formation.
![[Pasted image 20261027212115.png]]
    

#### Important Trends

- The maximum number of oxidation states is generally observed near the middle of a transition series.
    
- The highest oxidation state increases towards the middle and then decreases.
    
- Higher oxidation states are commonly stabilised by oxygen and fluorine.
    
- Lower oxidation states are often stabilised by ligands such as CO.
    

Examples:

- $MnO_4^-$ contains Mn in the $+7$ oxidation state.
    
- $Cr_2O_7^{2-}$ contains Cr in the $+6$ oxidation state.
    
- $Fe(CO)_5$ contains Fe in the zero oxidation state.

#### Why Is the Standard Electrode Potential of Copper Positive?

Copper has a positive standard electrode potential for the $Cu^{2+}/Cu$ couple.

The energy required for atomisation and ionisation of copper is not sufficiently compensated by the hydration enthalpy of $Cu^{2+}$.

Therefore, copper does not readily displace hydrogen from dilute non-oxidising acids.

### 3.6 Magnetic Properties

Transition elements show magnetic behaviour because of the presence of unpaired d-electrons.

#### Paramagnetic Substances

Substances containing one or more unpaired electrons are paramagnetic.

They are attracted by an external magnetic field.

Examples: $Fe^{3+}$, $Mn^{2+}$ and $Cu^{2+}$.

#### Diamagnetic Substances

Substances in which all electrons are paired are diamagnetic.

They are weakly repelled by a magnetic field.

Examples: $Sc^{3+}$, $Ti^{4+}$ and $Zn^{2+}$.

#### Magnetic Moment

For many first-row transition-metal ions, the spin-only magnetic moment is calculated using:

$\mu=\sqrt{n(n+2)}\ BM$

Where:

- $\mu$ = magnetic moment.
    
- $n$ = number of unpaired electrons.
    
- BM = Bohr magneton.
    

Examples:

- 1 unpaired electron: $\mu=\sqrt{3}=1.73\ BM$
    
- 2 unpaired electrons: $\mu=\sqrt{8}=2.83\ BM$
    
- 3 unpaired electrons: $\mu=\sqrt{15}=3.87\ BM$
    
- 4 unpaired electrons: $\mu=\sqrt{24}=4.90\ BM$
    
- 5 unpaired electrons: $\mu=\sqrt{35}=5.92\ BM$
    

**Exam point:** Always determine the electronic configuration of the ion before counting its unpaired electrons.

### 3.7 Formation of Coloured Ions

Many transition-metal ions form coloured compounds because of electronic transitions between split d-orbitals.

When a transition-metal ion is surrounded by ligands, its d-orbitals split into groups of different energies.

An electron can absorb visible light and move from a lower-energy d-orbital to a higher-energy d-orbital. This is called a **d–d transition**.

The remaining transmitted or reflected light gives the observed colour.

#### Important Examples

| Ion                           | Typical colour                             |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| $Ti^{3+}$                     | Purple                                     |
| $Cr^{3+}$                     | Green or violet, depending on the compound |
| $MnO_4^-$                     | Purple                                     |
| $Cr_2O_7^{2-}$                | Orange                                     |
| $Cu^{2+}$ in aqueous solution | Blue                                       |
| $Ni^{2+}$ in aqueous solution | Green                                      |

#### Why Are $d^0$ and $d^{10}$ Ions Usually Colourless?

- $d^0$ ions have no d-electrons available for d–d transitions.
    
- $d^{10}$ ions have completely filled d-orbitals and cannot undergo ordinary d–d transitions.

Examples: $Sc^{3+}$, $Ti^{4+}$, $Cu^+$ and $Zn^{2+}$.

Some compounds of these ions can still be coloured because of charge-transfer transitions or other effects.

### 3.8 Catalytic Properties

Transition metals and their compounds often act as catalysts because they can:

- Show variable oxidation states.
    
- Form intermediate compounds.
    
- Adsorb reactants on their surfaces.
    

Examples:

- Fe in the Haber process.
    
- $V_2O_5$ in the Contact process.
    
- Ni in the hydrogenation of vegetable oils.
    
- $MnO_2$ in the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide.
    

### 3.9 Formation of Complex Compounds

Transition metals readily form complex compounds because their ions are generally small, have relatively high charge density and can accept electron pairs from ligands.

Examples:

- $[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}$
    
- $[Cu(NH_3)_4]^{2+}$
    
- $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$
    

### 3.10 Formation of Interstitial Compounds

Small atoms such as H, C and N occupy the vacant spaces in the crystal lattices of transition metals.

These are called **interstitial compounds**.

Examples: $TiC$, $Fe_3C$ and some metal hydrides.

#### Properties

- They are generally hard.
    
- They often have high melting points.
    
- They retain metallic conductivity.
    
- They are usually non-stoichiometric.
    

### 3.11 Alloy Formation

Transition metals readily form alloys because their atomic sizes are often similar.

Atoms of one metal can replace atoms of another metal in the crystal lattice.

Examples:

- Stainless steel: iron, chromium and nickel.
    
- Brass: copper and zinc.
    
- Bronze: copper and tin.
    

## 4. Some Important Compounds of Transition Elements

### 4.1 Oxides of Transition Metals

Transition metals form oxides in different oxidation states.

The nature of the oxides depends on the oxidation state of the metal.

- Lower oxidation states generally form basic oxides.
    
- Intermediate oxidation states may form amphoteric oxides.
    
- Higher oxidation states generally form acidic oxides.
    

Examples:

- $MnO$ is basic.
    
- $Cr_2O_3$ is amphoteric.
    
- $CrO_3$ is acidic.
    
- $Mn_2O_7$ is acidic.
    

### 4.2 Potassium Dichromate — $K_2Cr_2O_7$

Potassium dichromate is an important orange-coloured oxidising agent.

#### Preparation

It is prepared from chromite ore, $FeCr_2O_4$.

**Step 1: Conversion into sodium chromate**

Finely powdered chromite ore is heated with sodium carbonate in the presence of air.

$4FeCr_2O_4+8Na_2CO_3+7O_2\rightarrow8Na_2CrO_4+2Fe_2O_3+8CO_2$

The sodium chromate formed is yellow.

**Step 2: Conversion into sodium dichromate**
Sodium chromate is acidified with sulphuric acid.

$2Na_2CrO_4+H_2SO_4\rightarrow Na_2Cr_2O_7+Na_2SO_4+H_2O$

**Step 3: Conversion into potassium dichromate**
Potassium chloride is added to sodium dichromate solution.

$Na_2Cr_2O_7+2KCl\rightarrow K_2Cr_2O_7+2NaCl$

Potassium dichromate crystallises out because it is less soluble(primary standard) than sodium dichromate under the relevant conditions.

#### Chromate–Dichromate Equilibrium

Chromate ions are yellow, whereas dichromate ions are orange.

In an acidic medium:

$2CrO_4^{2-}+2H^+\rightleftharpoons Cr_2O_7^{2-}+H_2O$

In an alkaline medium:

$Cr_2O_7^{2-}+2OH^-\rightleftharpoons2CrO_4^{2-}+H_2O$

**Colour change:**

- Yellow chromate → orange dichromate on acidification.
    
- Orange dichromate → yellow chromate on adding alkali.
    

#### Oxidising Action of Potassium Dichromate

In an acidic medium, dichromate ions are reduced from Cr(VI) to Cr(III).

The reduction half-equation is:

$Cr_2O_7^{2-}+14H^++6e^-\rightarrow2Cr^{3+}+7H_2O$

The orange dichromate solution turns green as $Cr^{3+}$ forms.

**Reaction with iron(II) ions**

$Cr_2O_7^{2-}+14H^++6Fe^{2+}\rightarrow2Cr^{3+}+7H_2O+6Fe^{3+}$

**Reaction with iodide ions**

$Cr_2O_7^{2-}+14H^++6I^-\rightarrow2Cr^{3+}+7H_2O+3I_2$

**Reaction with Chlorine (non redox)
$Cr_2O_7^{2-}+H_{2}SO_{4}+Cl^-(salt)\to CrO_{2}Cl_{2}\uparrow+H_{2}O$

## 4.3 Potassium Permanganate — $KMnO_4$

Potassium permanganate is a deep purple-coloured compound and a powerful oxidising agent.
 
#### Preparation

It is prepared from pyrolusite ore, $MnO_2$.

**Step 1: Formation of potassium manganate**

$2MnO_2+4KOH+O_2\rightarrow2K_2MnO_4+2H_2O$

Potassium manganate, $K_2MnO_4$, is green.

**Step 2: Conversion into potassium permanganate**

Potassium manganate is converted into potassium permanganate by oxidation, commonly by electrolytic methods.

$MnO_4^{2-}\rightarrow MnO_4^-+e^-$

The oxidation state of manganese increases from $+6$ to $+7$.

#### Oxidising Action in Different Media

The product formed depends on the reaction medium.

**Acidic medium**

Permanganate is reduced to $Mn^{2+}$.

$MnO_4^-+8H^++5e^-\rightarrow Mn^{2+}+4H_2O$

**Neutral or weakly alkaline medium**

Permanganate is commonly reduced to brown $MnO_2$.

$MnO_4^-+2H_2O+3e^-\rightarrow MnO_2+4OH^-$

**Strongly alkaline medium**

Permanganate can be reduced to green manganate ions.

$MnO_4^-+e^-\rightarrow MnO_4^{2-}$

#### Important Reactions of $KMnO_4$

**1. Oxidation of iron(II) ions in acidic medium**

$MnO_4^-+8H^++5Fe^{2+}\rightarrow Mn^{2+}+4H_2O+5Fe^{3+}$

**2. Oxidation of oxalate ions in acidic medium**

$2MnO_4^-+16H^++5C_2O_4^{2-}\rightarrow2Mn^{2+}+8H_2O+10CO_2$

**3. Oxidation of iodide ions in acidic medium**

$2MnO_4^-+16H^++10I^-\rightarrow2Mn^{2+}+8H_2O+5I_2$

**4. Oxidation of sulphite ions in acidic medium**

$2MnO_4^-+6H^++5SO_3^{2-}\rightarrow2Mn^{2+}+3H_2O+5SO_4^{2-}$
