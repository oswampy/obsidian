
## 1. Introduction to Coordination Compounds

### What Are Coordination Compounds?

**Coordination compounds** are compounds in which a central metal atom or ion is surrounded by a fixed number of ions or molecules called ligands.

The ligands are attached to the central metal atom or ion through coordinate bonds.

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_6]Cl_3$

In this compound:

- Central metal ion: $Co^{3+}$
    
- Ligands: $NH_3$
    
- Coordination number: 6
    
- Coordination sphere: $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$
    
- Counter ions: $3Cl^-$
    

### Important Terms

#### 1. Central Metal Atom or Ion

The metal atom or ion to which ligands are attached.

Examples:

- $Co^{3+}$
    
- $Fe^{2+}$
    
- $Cu^{2+}$
    
- $Ni^{2+}$
    

#### 2. Ligands

Ligands are ions or molecules that donate at least one lone pair of electrons to the central metal atom or ion.

Examples:

- $NH_3$
    
- $H_2O$
    
- $Cl^-$
    
- $CN^-$
    
- $CO$
    

The ligand acts as a Lewis base, while the central metal ion acts as a Lewis acid.

#### 3. Coordination Sphere

The central metal atom or ion and the ligands directly attached to it are collectively called the coordination sphere.

It is written inside square brackets.

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_6]Cl_3$

Coordination sphere: $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$

#### 4. Coordination Number

The coordination number is the total number of donor atoms directly bonded to the central metal atom or ion.

Examples:

- $[Ag(NH_3)_2]^+$: Coordination number = 2.
    
- $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$: Coordination number = 6.
    
- $[Fe(C_2O_4)_3]^{3-}$: Coordination number = 6.
    

**Important:** Coordination number is not necessarily equal to the number of ligands. A bidentate ligand contributes two donor atoms.

#### 5. Coordination Polyhedron

The spatial arrangement of ligands around the central metal ion is called the coordination polyhedron.

Common geometries:

- Coordination number 2: Linear.
    
- Coordination number 4: Tetrahedral or square planar.
    
- Coordination number 6: Octahedral.
    

#### 6. Oxidation Number

The oxidation number of the central metal ion is calculated using the total charge of the coordination entity.

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_5Cl]Cl_2$

Let the oxidation state of cobalt be $x$.

$x+0(5)-1=+2$

$x=+3$

Therefore, oxidation state of cobalt = +3.

#### 7. Homoleptic Complexes

Complexes containing only one type of ligand are called homoleptic complexes.

Examples:

- $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$
    
- $[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}$
    

#### 8. Heteroleptic Complexes

Complexes containing more than one type of ligand are called heteroleptic complexes.

Examples:

- $[Co(NH_3)_4Cl_2]^+$
    
- $[Pt(NH_3)_2Cl_2]$

- Oxygen: Nitrito.

$SCN^-$

- Sulphur: Thiocyanato-S.
    
- Nitrogen: Thiocyanato-N.
    

**Important:** Ambidentate ligands can produce linkage isomerism.

## 2.51 Classical and Non-Classical Ligands

### 1. Classical Ligands

Classical ligands are ligands that donate a lone pair of electrons from a donor atom to the central metal ion, forming a coordinate bond.

They generally act as Lewis bases.

Examples:

| Ligand   | Donor atom |
| -------- | ---------- |
| $NH_3$   | N          |
| $H_2O$   | O          |
| $Cl^-$   | Cl         |
| $CN^-$   | C or N     |
| $OH^-$   | O          |
| $en$     | N          |
| $C_2O_4$ | O          |

### 2. Non-Classical Ligands

Non-classical ligands are ligands that form coordinate bonds through their delocalised electrons, usually involving π-electron systems or multiple bonds.

They can coordinate through their π-electron clouds rather than only through a lone pair on a single donor atom.

Examples:

|Ligand|Name|Type of bonding|
|---|---|---|
|$C_2H_4$|Ethylene|π-bond coordination|
|$C_6H_6$|Benzene|π-electron coordination|
|$C_5H_5^-$|Cyclopentadienyl|π-electron coordination|
|$C_3H_5^-$|Allyl|π-electron coordination|
|$CO$|Carbon monoxide|σ-donation and π-back bonding|

### 3. Important Difference

|Classical ligands|Non-classical ligands|
|---|---|
|Usually donate a lone pair from a donor atom|Coordinate through delocalised π-electrons or π systems|
|Generally form coordinate σ-bonds|Often involve π interactions|
|Examples: $NH_3$, $H_2O$, $Cl^-$|Examples: $C_2H_4$, $C_6H_6$, $C_5H_5^-$|

Note: CO is a special case: it coordinates through carbon by σ-donation and also accepts electron density through π-back bonding. Its bonding is not simply a π-electron-cloud coordination like ethylene or benzene.
## 3. Werner's Theory of Coordination Compounds

### 3.1 Introduction

Alfred Werner proposed the coordination theory to explain the structures and properties of coordination compounds.

According to Werner, metals exhibit two types of valencies.

### 3.2 Primary Valency

Primary valency corresponds to the oxidation state of the central metal ion.

Properties:

- It is ionisable.
    
- It is usually satisfied by negative ions.
    
- It is non-directional.
    

### 3.3 Secondary Valency

Secondary valency corresponds to the coordination number of the central metal ion.

Properties:

- It is non-ionisable.
    
- It is satisfied by ligands.
    
- It is directional.
    
- It determines the geometry of the complex.
    

### 3.4 Werner's Explanation Using Cobalt Complexes

Consider the following compounds:

|Compound|AgCl precipitated with excess $AgNO_3$|Coordination number|
|---|---|---|
|$CoCl_3\cdot6NH_3$|3 mol|6|
|$CoCl_3\cdot5NH_3$|2 mol|6|
|$CoCl_3\cdot4NH_3$|1 mol|6|
|$CoCl_3\cdot3NH_3$|0 mol|6|

Their structures are:

1. $[Co(NH_3)_6]Cl_3$
    
2. $[Co(NH_3)_5Cl]Cl_2$
    
3. $[Co(NH_3)_4Cl_2]Cl$
    
4. $[Co(NH_3)_3Cl_3]$
    

### 3.5 Important Conclusions

- Chloride ions outside the coordination sphere are ionisable.
    
- Chloride ions inside the coordination sphere are directly bonded to cobalt.
    
- All four complexes have coordination number 6.
    
- The primary valency of cobalt is 3.
    
- The secondary valency of cobalt is 6.
    

## 4. Nomenclature of Coordination Compounds

### 4.1 Basic Rules

The IUPAC naming of coordination compounds follows a specific order.

#### Rule 1: Name the Cation Before the Anion

For compounds containing both positive and negative ions, name the cation first and then the anion.

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_6]Cl_3$

Hexaamminecobalt(III) chloride.

#### Rule 2: Name Ligands Before the Central Metal

Within the coordination sphere, ligands are named before the metal.

#### Rule 3: Arrange Ligands Alphabetically

Different ligands are arranged alphabetically according to their ligand names.

Prefixes such as di-, tri-, tetra- are ignored when arranging ligands alphabetically.

#### Rule 4: Indicate the Number of Ligands

Use prefixes:

- 2: Di
    
- 3: Tri
    
- 4: Tetra
    
- 5: Penta
    
- 6: Hexa
    

For complicated ligand names or polydentate ligands, use:

- Bis
    
- Tris
    
- Tetrakis
    

The ligand name is placed in parentheses.

#### Rule 5: Naming the Central Metal

If the coordination entity is positively charged or neutral, the normal metal name is used.

If the coordination entity is negatively charged, the metal name ends in -ate.

Examples:

- Iron → Ferrate
    
- Copper → Cuprate
    
- Silver → Argentate
    
- Gold → Aurate
    
- Tin → Stannate
    
- Lead → Plumbate
    

#### Rule 6: Oxidation State

The oxidation state of the central metal is written in Roman numerals inside parentheses.

Example:

Cobalt(III).

### 4.2 Important Ligand Names

|Formula|IUPAC ligand name|
|---|---|

#### Example 1

$[Co(NH_3)_6]Cl_3$

- Ligand: Ammine.

- Oxidation state: +3.

Name: Pentaamminechloridocobalt(III) chloride.

#### Example 3

$x+6(-1)=-4$

$x=+2$

$[Fe(CN)_6]$

$+3+6(-1)=-3$

Step 3: Add potassium ions to balance charge.

$K_3[Fe(CN)_6]$

## 5. Isomerism in Coordination Compounds

### 5.1 What Is Isomerism?

Isomerism is the phenomenon in which compounds have the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms or ligands.

Coordination compounds show two major types of isomerism:

1. Structural isomerism.
    
2. Stereoisomerism.
    

### 5.2 Structural Isomerism

Structural isomers differ in the connectivity of atoms or the way ligands are attached.

Types:

1. Ionisation isomerism.
    
2. Hydrate or solvate isomerism.
    
3. Linkage isomerism.
    
4. Coordination isomerism.
    

### 5.3 Ionisation Isomerism

Ionisation isomerism occurs when an ion inside the coordination sphere exchanges places with an ion outside the coordination sphere.

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_5Br]SO_4$

and

$[Co(NH_3)_5SO_4]Br$

The first produces sulphate ions in solution, whereas the second produces bromide ions.

### 5.4 Hydrate Isomerism

Hydrate isomerism occurs when water molecules inside and outside the coordination sphere exchange positions.

Example:

$[Cr(H_2O)_6]Cl_3$

$[Cr(H_2O)_5Cl]Cl_2\cdot H_2O$

$[Cr(H_2O)_4Cl_2]Cl\cdot2H_2O$

These compounds contain the same overall composition but differ in the distribution of water molecules.

### 5.5 Linkage Isomerism

Linkage isomerism occurs when an ambidentate ligand coordinates through different donor atoms.

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_5(NO_2)]Cl_2$

Possible forms:

- Nitro form: Coordination through nitrogen.
    
- Nitrito form: Coordination through oxygen.
    

### 5.6 Coordination Isomerism

Coordination isomerism occurs when ligands are exchanged between the complex cation and complex anion.

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_6][Cr(CN)_6]$

and

$[Cr(NH_3)_6][Co(CN)_6]$

### 5.7 Stereoisomerism

Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but different spatial arrangements.

Types:

1. Geometrical isomerism.
    
2. Optical isomerism.
    

### 5.8 Geometrical Isomerism

Geometrical isomerism occurs due to different spatial arrangements of ligands around the central metal ion.

#### Cis and Trans Isomerism

**Cis isomer:**

Similar ligands are adjacent to each other.

**Trans isomer:**

Similar ligands are opposite to each other.

Example:

$[Pt(NH_3)_2Cl_2]$

<ins>Square planar geometry</ins>

- Cisplatin: Two $NH_3$ ligands are adjacent.
    
- Transplatin: Two $NH_3$ ligands are opposite.
    

Other examples:

$[Co(NH_3)_4Cl_2]^+$

This octahedral complex has cis and trans forms.

### 5.9 Geometrical Isomerism in Octahedral Complexes

#### Type 1: $MA_4B_2$

Two geometrical isomers are possible:

- Cis.
    
- Trans.
    

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_4Cl_2]^+$

#### Type 2: $MA_3B_3$

Two geometrical isomers are possible:

- Facial (fac).
    
- Meridional (mer).
    

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_3Cl_3]$

**Facial Isomer:**

Three identical ligands occupy one triangular face of the octahedron. All three identical ligands are mutually cis.

**Meridional Isomer:**

Three identical ligands lie along a meridian plane. Two of the identical ligands are trans.

#### Type 3: $MA_2B_4$

Two geometrical isomers are possible:

- Cis.
    
- Trans.
    

#### Type 4: $M(AA)_2B_2$

Two geometrical arrangements are possible:

- Cis.
    
- Trans.
    

Here, $AA$ represents a bidentate ligand.

The cis form can be optically active.

### 5.10 Optical Isomerism

Optical isomerism occurs when compounds exist as non-superimposable mirror images.

These mirror-image forms are called enantiomers.

They rotate plane-polarised light in opposite directions.

- Dextrorotatory: Rotates light clockwise.
    
- Laevorotatory: Rotates light anticlockwise.
    

#### Examples

$[Co(en)_3]^{3+}$

This complex exists as two optical isomers.

$[Cr(ox)_3]^{3-}$

This complex also exhibits optical isomerism.

#### Important Points

- Optical isomers are non-superimposable mirror images.
    
- They have identical physical properties in an achiral environment except for their optical rotation.
    
- A racemic mixture contains equal amounts of both enantiomers and is optically inactive.
    

## 6. Valence Bond Theory (VBT)

### 6.1 Introduction

Valence Bond Theory explains the bonding, hybridisation, geometry and magnetic properties of coordination compounds.

According to VBT:

- Ligands donate lone pairs of electrons.
    
- The central metal ion provides vacant orbitals.
    
- These orbitals hybridise to form equivalent hybrid orbitals.
    
- Ligands donate electron pairs into these hybrid orbitals.
    

### 6.2 Types of Hybridisation

|Hybridisation|Geometry|
|---|---|
|$sp$|Linear|
|$sp^3$|Tetrahedral|
|$dsp^2$|Square planar|
|$sp^3d^2$|Outer orbital octahedral|
|$d^2sp^3$|Inner orbital octahedral|

### 6.3 Inner Orbital Complexes

Inner orbital complexes use inner $(n-1)d$ orbitals for hybridisation.

For coordination number 6:

Hybridisation:

$d^2sp^3$

Geometry:

Octahedral.

These are generally called low-spin complexes when the ligand causes pairing of d-electrons before hybridisation.

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$

Cobalt:

$Co^{3+}:3d^6$

The electrons pair up, leaving two vacant 3d orbitals.

Hybridisation:

$d^2sp^3$

The complex is diamagnetic.

### 6.4 Outer Orbital Complexes

Outer orbital complexes use outer nd orbitals for hybridisation.

For coordination number 6:

Hybridisation:

$sp^3d^2$

Geometry:

Octahedral.

These are generally high-spin complexes.

Example:

$[CoF_6]^{3-}$

Cobalt:

$Co^{3+}:3d^6$

$F^-$ is a weak-field ligand, so pairing does not occur in the 3d orbitals before hybridisation.

Hybridisation:

$sp^3d^2$

The complex has four unpaired electrons and is paramagnetic.

### 6.5 VBT Examples

#### Example 1: $[Ni(CN)_4]^{2-}$

Step 1: Oxidation state.

$x+4(-1)=-2$

$x=+2$

Step 2: Electronic configuration.

$Ni^{2+}=[Ar]3d^8$

Step 3: Ligand strength.

$CN^-$ is a strong-field ligand.

Step 4: Pairing.

The electrons pair up to create one vacant 3d orbital.

Step 5: Hybridisation.

$dsp^2$

Geometry: Square planar.

Magnetic nature: Diamagnetic.

#### Example 2: $[NiCl_4]^{2-}$

Step 1: Oxidation state.

$Ni^{2+}$

Step 2: Electronic configuration.

$3d^8$

Step 3: Ligand strength.

$Cl^-$ is a weak-field ligand.

Step 4: Hybridisation.

$sp^3$

Geometry: Tetrahedral.

Magnetic nature: Paramagnetic with two unpaired electrons.

#### Example 3: $[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}$

Oxidation state:

$x+6(-1)=-4$

$x=+2$

Electronic configuration:

$Fe^{2+}=[Ar]3d^6$

$CN^-$ is a strong-field ligand.

Electrons pair up.

Hybridisation:

$d^2sp^3$

Geometry: Octahedral.

Magnetic nature: Diamagnetic.

#### Example 4: $[FeF_6]^{3-}$

Oxidation state:

$x+6(-1)=-3$

$x=+3$

Electronic configuration:

$Fe^{3+}=[Ar]3d^5$

$F^-$ is a weak-field ligand.

No pairing occurs in the 3d orbitals.

Hybridisation:

$sp^3d^2$

Geometry: Octahedral.

Magnetic nature: Paramagnetic with five unpaired electrons.

### 6.6 Limitations of VBT

- It cannot explain the colours of coordination compounds.
    
- It does not explain the relative strengths of ligands.
    
- It cannot satisfactorily explain the detailed electronic spectra.
    
- It does not provide a quantitative explanation of stability.
    
- It does not fully explain why certain complexes are high spin or low spin.
    

## 7. Crystal Field Theory (CFT)

### 7.1 Introduction

Crystal Field Theory explains the bonding and properties of coordination compounds by considering the electrostatic interaction between the central metal ion and ligands.

According to CFT:

- Ligands are treated as point charges or dipoles.
    
- The d-orbitals of the free metal ion initially have equal energy.
    
- When ligands approach the metal ion, electrostatic repulsion causes splitting of d-orbital energies.
    

This splitting is called crystal field splitting.

### 7.2 Degenerate d-Orbitals

In a free gaseous metal ion, all five d-orbitals have the same energy.

They are called degenerate orbitals.

The five d-orbitals are:

1. $d_{xy}$
    
2. $d_{yz}$
    
3. $d_{xz}$
    
4. $d_{x^2-y^2}$
    
5. $d_{z^2}$
    

### 7.3 Crystal Field Splitting in Octahedral Complexes

In an octahedral complex, six ligands approach the central metal ion along the x, y and z axes.

The orbitals pointing directly towards the ligands experience greater repulsion.

Therefore, the d-orbitals split into two groups.

#### Higher Energy Orbitals: $e_g$

- $d_{x^2-y^2}$
    
- $d_{z^2}$
    

These orbitals point directly towards the ligands.

#### Lower Energy Orbitals: $t_{2g}$

- $d_{xy}$
    
- $d_{yz}$
    
- $d_{xz}$
    

These orbitals lie between the axes.

The energy difference between these groups is called octahedral crystal field splitting energy.

$\Delta_o$

### 7.4 Energy of Orbitals in an Octahedral Field

Relative to the barycentre:

- Each $t_{2g}$ orbital has energy $-0.4\Delta_o$.
    
- Each $e_g$ orbital has energy $+0.6\Delta_o$.
    

Total energy remains unchanged:

$3(-0.4\Delta_o)+2(+0.6\Delta_o)=0$

### 7.5 Crystal Field Splitting in Tetrahedral Complexes

In tetrahedral complexes, four ligands approach the metal ion between the coordinate axes.

The splitting pattern is opposite to that of an octahedral complex.

#### Lower Energy Orbitals: $e$

- $d_{x^2-y^2}$
    
- $d_{z^2}$
    

#### Higher Energy Orbitals: $t_2$

- $d_{xy}$
    
- $d_{yz}$
    
- $d_{xz}$
    

### 7.6 Energy of Orbitals in a Tetrahedral Field

Relative to the barycentre:

- Each $e$ orbital has energy $-0.6\Delta_t$.
    
- Each $t_2$ orbital has energy $+0.4\Delta_t$.
    

The tetrahedral splitting is smaller than the octahedral splitting.

$\Delta_t=\dfrac{4}{9}\Delta_o$

### 7.7 Why Is Tetrahedral Splitting Smaller?

- Tetrahedral complexes have only four ligands.
    
- The ligands do not approach directly along the coordinate axes.
    
- The overall repulsion between ligands and d-electrons is smaller.
    

Therefore:

$\Delta_t<\Delta_o$

### 7.8 Square Planar Splitting

In square planar complexes, four ligands approach the central metal ion along the x and y axes.

The typical order of d-orbital energies from highest to lowest is:

$ d_{x^2-y^2}>d_{xy}>d_{z^2}>d_{xz}=d_{yz}$

The exact splitting depends on the metal ion and ligands.

Square planar complexes are commonly associated with $d^8$ metal ions.

Example:

$[Ni(CN)_4]^{2-}$

### 7.9 Strong-Field and Weak-Field Ligands

**Strong-field ligands**

Produce relatively large crystal field splitting.

Examples:

- $CN^-$
    
- CO
    
- $NO_2^-$
    
- en
    

**Weak-field ligands**

Produce relatively small crystal field splitting.

Examples:

- $I^-$
    
- $Br^-$
    
- $Cl^-$
    
- $F^-$
    
- $H_2O$
    

### 7.10 Spectrochemical Series

The spectrochemical series arranges ligands in increasing order of crystal field splitting strength.

$I^-<Br^-<SCN^-<Cl^-<S^{2-}<F^-<OH^-<C_2O_4^{2-}<H_2O<NCS^-<CH_3CN<NH_3<en<CN^-<CO$

**Important:** The position of an ambidentate ligand depends on the donor atom.

### 7.11 High-Spin and Low-Spin Complexes

The distribution of electrons depends on the competition between:

- Crystal field splitting energy ($\Delta$).
    
- Pairing energy ($P$).
    

#### High-Spin Complexes

When:

$\Delta_o<P$

Electrons prefer to occupy higher-energy orbitals rather than pair up in lower-energy orbitals.

Properties:

- More unpaired electrons.
    
- Usually paramagnetic.
    
- Common with weak-field ligands.
    

#### Low-Spin Complexes

When:

$\Delta_o>P$

Electrons prefer to pair up in lower-energy orbitals.

Properties:

- Fewer unpaired electrons.
    
- May be diamagnetic or paramagnetic.
    
- Common with strong-field ligands.
    

### 7.12 Electron Configuration in Octahedral Complexes

For a $d^4$ metal ion:

**Weak-field case:**

$t_{2g}^3e_g^1$

Four unpaired electrons.

**Strong-field case:**

$t_{2g}^4e_g^0$

Two unpaired electrons.

For a $d^6$ metal ion:

**Weak-field case:**

$t_{2g}^4e_g^2$

Four unpaired electrons.

**Strong-field case:**

$t_{2g}^6e_g^0$

Zero unpaired electrons.

### 7.13 Crystal Field Stabilisation Energy (CFSE)

CFSE is the lowering in energy of d-electrons due to crystal field splitting relative to the barycentre.

For an octahedral complex:

$CFSE=(-0.4n_{t_{2g}}+0.6n_{e_g})\Delta_o$

Where:

- $n_{t_{2g}}$ = number of electrons in lower orbitals.
    
- $n_{e_g}$ = number of electrons in higher orbitals.
    

#### Examples

**$d^1$ configuration**

$t_{2g}^1$

$CFSE=-0.4\Delta_o$

**$d^2$ configuration**

$t_{2g}^2$

$CFSE=-0.8\Delta_o$

**$d^3$ configuration**

$t_{2g}^3$

$CFSE=-1.2\Delta_o$

**$d^6$ low-spin configuration**

$t_{2g}^6$

$CFSE=-2.4\Delta_o$

**$d^6$ high-spin configuration**

$t_{2g}^4e_g^2$

$CFSE=-0.4\Delta_o$

Pairing energy must also be considered when comparing configurations.

### 7.14 CFSE in Tetrahedral Complexes

For tetrahedral complexes:

$CFSE=(-0.6n_e+0.4n_{t_2})\Delta_t$

Where:

- $n_e$ = electrons in lower-energy orbitals.
    
- $n_{t_2}$ = electrons in higher-energy orbitals.
    

Most tetrahedral complexes are high spin because $\Delta_t$ is generally smaller than pairing energy.

## 8. Magnetic Properties of Coordination Compounds

### 8.1 Paramagnetic Complexes

Complexes containing one or more unpaired electrons are paramagnetic.

They are attracted by an external magnetic field.

Examples:

- $[FeF_6]^{3-}$
    
- $[CoF_6]^{3-}$
    
- $[NiCl_4]^{2-}$
    

### 8.2 Diamagnetic Complexes

Complexes in which all electrons are paired are diamagnetic.

They are weakly repelled by an external magnetic field.

Examples:

- $[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}$
    
- $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$
    
- $[Ni(CN)_4]^{2-}$
    

### 8.3 Magnetic Moment

The spin-only magnetic moment is calculated using:

$\mu=\sqrt{n(n+2)}\ BM$

Where $n$ is the number of unpaired electrons.

## 9. Colour of Coordination Compounds

### 9.1 Why Are Coordination Compounds Coloured?

Many coordination compounds are coloured because of electronic transitions between split d-orbitals.

When white light falls on a complex:

- Certain wavelengths are absorbed.
    
- Electrons gain energy and move to higher-energy d-orbitals.
    
- The remaining transmitted or reflected light produces the observed colour.
    

This is called a d–d transition.

### 9.2 Relation Between Colour and Crystal Field Splitting

The energy absorbed is:

$\Delta= h\nu$

Since:

$\nu=\dfrac{c}{\lambda}$

Therefore:

$\Delta=\dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$

Hence:

- Larger splitting means higher absorbed frequency.
    
- Larger splitting means shorter absorbed wavelength.
    

### 9.3 Why Are $d^0$ and $d^{10}$ Complexes Usually Colourless?

$d^0$ complexes:

There are no d-electrons available for ordinary d–d transitions.

$d^{10}$ complexes:

All d-orbitals are completely filled, so ordinary d–d transitions are not possible.

Examples:

- $Sc^{3+}$: $d^0$
    
- $Ti^{4+}$: $d^0$
    
- $Zn^{2+}$: $d^{10}$
    
- $Cu^+$: $d^{10}$
    

Some compounds can still show colour due to charge-transfer transitions.

### 9.4 Charge Transfer Transitions

In certain complexes, electrons move between ligand and metal orbitals.

These are called charge-transfer transitions.

They can produce intense colours even when ordinary d–d transitions are not possible.

## 10. Stability of Coordination Compounds

### 10.1 Stability Constant

The stability of a coordination complex is expressed using its formation or stability constant.

For:

$M+nL\rightleftharpoons ML_n$

The formation constant is:

$\beta_n=\dfrac{[ML_n]}{[M][L]^n}$

A higher value of $\beta_n$ indicates greater thermodynamic stability under the specified conditions.

### 10.2 Stepwise Formation Constants

Complex formation may occur in multiple steps.

$M+L\rightleftharpoons ML$

$ML+L\rightleftharpoons ML_2$

The stepwise stability constants are:

$K_1=\dfrac{[ML]}{[M][L]}$

$K_2=\dfrac{[ML_2]}{[ML][L]}$

Overall stability constant:

$\beta_2=K_1K_2$

### 10.3 Chelate Effect

Chelating ligands generally form more stable complexes than comparable complexes containing equivalent monodentate ligands.

This increased stability is called the chelate effect.

Examples:

- $[Cu(en)_2]^{2+}$
    
- $[Co(en)_3]^{3+}$
    

**Reason:** Chelation often produces a favourable entropy change because multiple monodentate ligands can be replaced by fewer multidentate ligands.

### 10.4 Factors Affecting Stability

- Charge on the central metal ion.
    
- Size of the central metal ion.
    
- Nature of the ligand.
    
- Denticity of the ligand.
    
- Crystal field stabilisation.
    
- Chelate formation.
    
- Steric effects.
    

## 11. Applications of Coordination Compounds

Contains iron in a coordination environment and is responsible for oxygen transport in blood.

Contains magnesium at its centre and plays an important role in photosynthesis.

Contains cobalt in its coordination structure.

Formula:

## 12. Important CBSE Reasoning Questions

### 12.1 Why Are Coordination Compounds Coloured?

Due to electronic transitions between split d-orbitals, which absorb certain wavelengths of visible light.

### 12.2 Why Are Most Coordination Compounds of Transition Metals Coloured?

Transition-metal ions often contain partially filled d-orbitals, allowing electronic transitions.

### 12.3 Why Are $[Ni(CN)_4]^{2-}$ and $[NiCl_4]^{2-}$ Different in Geometry?

$CN^-$ is a strong-field ligand and causes pairing, resulting in $dsp^2$ hybridisation and square planar geometry.

$Cl^-$ is a weak-field ligand and does not cause pairing, resulting in $sp^3$ hybridisation and tetrahedral geometry.

### 12.4 Why Is $[Ni(CN)_4]^{2-}$ Diamagnetic?

$Ni^{2+}$ has a $3d^8$ configuration. The strong-field ligand $CN^-$ causes electron pairing, leaving no unpaired electrons.

### 12.5 Why Is $[NiCl_4]^{2-}$ Paramagnetic?

$Cl^-$ is a weak-field ligand. The two unpaired electrons in the $3d^8$ configuration remain unpaired.

### 12.6 Why Do Coordination Compounds Show Isomerism?

Ligands can be arranged in different ways around the central metal ion, producing different connectivity or spatial arrangements.

### 12.7 Why Are Tetrahedral Complexes Usually High Spin?

The tetrahedral splitting energy is relatively small:

$\Delta_t=\dfrac{4}{9}\Delta_o$

It is generally smaller than pairing energy, so electrons occupy higher orbitals before pairing.

### 12.8 Why Is EDTA a Hexadentate Ligand?

EDTA can coordinate through six donor atoms: two nitrogen atoms and four oxygen atoms.

### 12.9 Why Are Chelate Complexes Generally More Stable?

Chelating ligands form ring structures with the metal ion, and complex formation often involves a favourable entropy change.

### 12.10 Why Is the Coordination Number Different from the Number of Ligands?

Because a ligand can coordinate through more than one donor atom.

For example:

$[Fe(C_2O_4)_3]^{3-}$

There are three ligands, but each oxalate ligand is bidentate.

Coordination number:

$3\times2=6$

## 15. Common JEE Exam Traps

### 15.1 Oxidation State and Coordination Number

- Oxidation state is not the same as coordination number.
    
- Neutral ligands contribute zero charge.
    
- Anionic ligands contribute negative charge.
    
- Bidentate ligands contribute two donor atoms.
    
- Always include the charge of the entire coordination entity.
    

### 15.2 Nomenclature

- Name the cation before the anion.
    
- Ligands are named before the metal.
    
- Arrange different ligand names alphabetically.
    
- Negative complex ions use metal names ending in -ate.
    
- Write the oxidation state in Roman numerals.
    
- Ammine has two m's; ammine is different from amine.
    

### 15.3 Isomerism

- Cis and trans forms are geometrical isomers.
    
- fac and mer forms occur in suitable octahedral complexes.
    
- Linkage isomerism requires an ambidentate ligand.
    
- Ionisation isomerism involves exchange of ions inside and outside the coordination sphere.
    
- Optical isomers are non-superimposable mirror images.
    

### 15.4 VBT

- Determine the oxidation state first.
    
- Write the d-electron configuration of the metal ion.
    
- Determine whether the ligand is strong or weak field.
    
- Decide whether pairing occurs.
    
- Only then determine hybridisation and magnetic nature.
    

### 15.5 CFT

- Octahedral: $t_{2g}$ lower, $e_g$ higher.
    
- Tetrahedral: $e$ lower, $t_2$ higher.
    
- $\Delta_t$ is smaller than $\Delta_o$.
    
- Strong-field ligands favour low-spin configurations.
    
- Weak-field ligands favour high-spin configurations.
    
- Always count unpaired electrons after filling the split orbitals.
    

### 15.6 Magnetic Properties

- Zero unpaired electrons means diamagnetic.
    
- One or more unpaired electrons means paramagnetic.
    
- Do not calculate magnetic moment using the number of d-electrons directly; use the number of unpaired electrons.
