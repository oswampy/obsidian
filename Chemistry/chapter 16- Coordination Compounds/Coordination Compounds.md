
## 1. Introduction to Coordination Compounds

### What Are Coordination Compounds?

**Coordination compounds** are compounds in which a central metal atom or ion is surrounded by a fixed number of ions or molecules called ligands.

The ligands are attached to the central metal atom or ion through coordinate bonds.

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_6]Cl_3$

In this compound:

- Central metal ion: $Co^{3+}$
    
- Ligands: $NH_3$
    
- Coordination number: 6
    
- Coordination sphere: $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$
    
- Counter ions: $3Cl^-$
    

### Important Terms

#### 1. Central Metal Atom or Ion

The metal atom or ion to which ligands are attached.

Examples:

- $Co^{3+}$
    
- $Fe^{2+}$
    
- $Cu^{2+}$
    
- $Ni^{2+}$
    

#### 2. Ligands

Ligands are ions or molecules that donate at least one lone pair of electrons to the central metal atom or ion.

Examples:

- $NH_3$
    
- $H_2O$
    
- $Cl^-$
    
- $CN^-$
    
- $CO$
    

The ligand acts as a Lewis base, while the central metal ion acts as a Lewis acid.

#### 3. Coordination Sphere

The central metal atom or ion and the ligands directly attached to it are collectively called the coordination sphere.

It is written inside square brackets.

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_6]Cl_3$

Coordination sphere: $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$

#### 4. Coordination Number⭐

The coordination number is the total number of donor atoms directly bonded to the central metal atom or ion.

Examples:

- $[Ag(NH_3)_2]^+$: Coordination number = 2.
    
- $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$: Coordination number = 6.
    
- $[Fe(C_2O_4)_3]^{3-}$: Coordination number = 6.
    

**Important:** Coordination number is not necessarily equal to the number of ligands. A bidentate ligand contributes two donor atoms.
![[Pasted image 20261108162322.png]]
#### 5. Coordination Polyhedron

The spatial arrangement of ligands around the central metal ion is called the coordination polyhedron.

Common geometries:

- Coordination number 2: Linear.
    
- Coordination number 4: Tetrahedral or square planar.
    
- Coordination number 6: Octahedral.
    

#### 6. Oxidation Number

The oxidation number of the central metal ion is calculated using the total charge of the coordination entity.

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_5Cl]Cl_2$

Let the oxidation state of cobalt be $x$.

$x+0(5)-1=+2$

$x=+3$

Therefore, oxidation state of cobalt = +3.

#### 7. Homoleptic Complexes

Complexes containing only one type of ligand are called homoleptic complexes.

Examples:

- $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$
    
- $[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}$
    

#### 8. Heteroleptic Complexes

Complexes containing more than one type of ligand are called heteroleptic complexes.

Examples:

- $[Co(NH_3)_4Cl_2]^+$
    
- $[Pt(NH_3)_2Cl_2]$

## 2.51 Classical and Non-Classical Ligands

### 1. Classical Ligands

Classical ligands are ligands that donate a lone pair of electrons from a donor atom to the central metal ion, forming a coordinate bond.

They generally act as Lewis bases.

Examples:

| Ligand   | Donor atom |
| -------- | ---------- |
| $NH_3$   | N          |
| $H_2O$   | O          |
| $Cl^-$   | Cl         |
| $CN^-$   | C or N     |
| $OH^-$   | O          |
| $en$     | N          |
| $C_2O_4$ | O          |

### 2. Non-Classical Ligands

Non-classical ligands are ligands that form coordinate bonds through their delocalised electrons, usually involving π-electron systems or multiple bonds.

They can coordinate through their π-electron clouds rather than only through a lone pair on a single donor atom.

Examples:

|Ligand|Name|Type of bonding|
|---|---|---|
|$C_2H_4$|Ethylene|π-bond coordination|
|$C_6H_6$|Benzene|π-electron coordination|
|$C_5H_5^-$|Cyclopentadienyl|π-electron coordination|
|$C_3H_5^-$|Allyl|π-electron coordination|
|$CO$|Carbon monoxide|σ-donation and π-back bonding|

### 3. Important Difference

|Classical ligands|Non-classical ligands|
|---|---|
|Usually donate a lone pair from a donor atom|Coordinate through delocalised π-electrons or π systems|
|Generally form coordinate σ-bonds|Often involve π interactions|
|Examples: $NH_3$, $H_2O$, $Cl^-$|Examples: $C_2H_4$, $C_6H_6$, $C_5H_5^-$|

Note: CO is a special case: it coordinates through carbon by σ-donation and also accepts electron density through π-back bonding. Its bonding is not simply a π-electron-cloud coordination like ethylene or benzene. IT is a sigma donor and pi acceptor

## 3. Werner's Theory of Coordination Compounds

### 3.1 Introduction

Alfred Werner proposed the coordination theory to explain the structures and properties of coordination compounds.

According to Werner, metals exhibit two types of valencies.

### 3.2 Primary Valency

Primary valency corresponds to the oxidation state of the central metal ion.

Properties:

- It is ionisable.
    
- It is usually satisfied by negative ions.
    
- It is non-directional.
    

### 3.3 Secondary Valency

Secondary valency corresponds to the coordination number of the central metal ion.

Properties:

- It is non-ionisable.
    
- It is satisfied by ligands.
    
- It is directional.
    
- It determines the geometry of the complex.

Consider the following compounds:

|$CoCl_3\cdot6NH_3$|3 mol|6|
|$CoCl_3\cdot5NH_3$|2 mol|6|
|$CoCl_3\cdot4NH_3$|1 mol|6|
|$CoCl_3\cdot3NH_3$|0 mol|6|

- Chloride ions outside the coordination sphere are ionisable.

## 4. Nomenclature of Coordination Compounds

### 4.1 Basic Rules

The IUPAC naming of coordination compounds follows a specific order.

#### Rule 1: Name the Cation Before the Anion

For compounds containing both positive and negative ions, name the cation first and then the anion.

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_6]Cl_3$

Hexaamminecobalt(III) chloride.

#### Rule 2: Name Ligands Before the Central Metal

Within the coordination sphere, ligands are named before the metal.

#### Rule 3: Arrange Ligands Alphabetically

Different ligands are arranged alphabetically according to their ligand names.

Prefixes such as di-, tri-, tetra- are ignored when arranging ligands alphabetically.

#### Rule 4: Indicate the Number of Ligands

Use prefixes:

- 2: Di
    
- 3: Tri
    
- 4: Tetra
    
- 5: Penta
    
- 6: Hexa
    

For complicated ligand names or polydentate ligands, use:

- Bis
    
- Tris
    
- Tetrakis
    

The ligand name is placed in parentheses.

#### Rule 5: Naming the Central Metal

If the coordination entity is positively charged or neutral, the normal metal name is used.

If the coordination entity is negatively charged, the metal name ends in -ate.

Examples:

- Iron → Ferrate
    
- Copper → Cuprate
    
- Silver → Argentate
    
- Gold → Aurate
    
- Tin → Stannate
    
- Lead → Plumbate
    

#### Rule 6: Oxidation State

The oxidation state of the central metal is written in Roman numerals inside parentheses.

Example:

Cobalt(III).

## Effective Atomic Number (EAN) and Sidgwick's Rule

### 1. Effective Atomic Number (EAN)

The Effective Atomic Number (EAN) is the total number of electrons surrounding the central metal atom or ion after accepting electron pairs from the ligands.

It represents the electron count of the central metal in a coordination complex.

### 2. Sidgwick's EAN Rule

According to Sidgwick's rule, the central metal ion tends to accept electron pairs from ligands until its effective atomic number becomes equal to the atomic number of the nearest noble gas.

In other words, the EAN of the central metal tends to attain the electronic configuration of the nearest noble gas.

### 3. EAN Formula

$EAN=Z-\text+2\times CN$

Where:

- $Z$ = Atomic number of central metal.
    
- Oxidation state = Oxidation state of central metal.
    
- $CN$ = Coordination number.
    
- Each donor atom donates one electron pair (2 electrons).
    

### 4. Examples of EAN Calculation

Example 1: $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$

- Atomic number of Co = 27
    
- Oxidation state = +3
    
- Coordination number = 6
    

$EAN=27-3+(2\times6)$

$EAN=36$

Nearest noble gas: Krypton (Kr).

Therefore, it follows the EAN rule.


Example 3: $[Ni(CO)_4]$

- Atomic number of Ni = 28
    
- Oxidation state = 0
    
- Coordination number = 4
    

$EAN=28-0+(2\times4)$

$EAN=36$

Nearest noble gas: Krypton (Kr).

Therefore, it follows the EAN rule.

### 5. Exceptions to Sidgwick's Rule

Not all coordination compounds follow the EAN rule. Many stable complexes have EAN values different from the atomic number of the nearest noble gas.

Examples:

| Complex            | EAN calculation | EAN | Noble gas configuration? |
| ------------------ | --------------- | --- | ------------------------ |
| $[Cu(NH_3)_4]$     | $29-2+8$        | 35  | No                       |
| $[Pt(NH_3)_2Cl_2]$ | $78-2+8$        | 84  | No                       |
| $[Fe(CN)_6]$       | $26-3+12$       | 35  | No                       |
| $[Ni(CN)_4]$       | $28-2+8$        | 34  | No                       |

### 6. Limitations of EAN Rule

1. Many stable coordination compounds do not follow the EAN rule.
    
2. It cannot explain the geometry of coordination compounds.
    
3. It cannot predict the magnetic properties of complexes.
    
4. It does not explain the colour of coordination compounds.
    
5. It is not sufficient to predict the stability of every coordination compound.

![[Pasted image 20261108194411.png]]

Conclusion: The EAN rule is a useful guideline for understanding electron counting in coordination compounds, but it is not a universal rule for their stability.


## 6. Valence Bond Theory (VBT)

### 6.1 Introduction

Valence Bond Theory explains the bonding, hybridisation, geometry and magnetic properties of coordination compounds.

According to VBT:

- Ligands donate lone pairs of electrons.
    
- The central metal ion provides vacant orbitals.
    
- These orbitals hybridise to form equivalent hybrid orbitals.
    
- Ligands donate electron pairs into these hybrid orbitals.
    

### 6.2 Types of Hybridisation

|Hybridisation|Geometry|
|---|---|
|$sp$|Linear|
|$sp^3$|Tetrahedral|
|$dsp^2$|Square planar|
|$sp^3d^2$|Outer orbital octahedral|
|$d^2sp^3$|Inner orbital octahedral|

### 6.3 Inner Orbital Complexes

Inner orbital complexes use inner $(n-1)d$ orbitals for hybridisation.

For coordination number 6:

Hybridisation:

$d^2sp^3$

Geometry:

Octahedral.

These are generally called low-spin complexes when the ligand causes pairing of d-electrons before hybridisation.

Example:

$[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$

Cobalt:

$Co^{3+}:3d^6$

The electrons pair up, leaving two vacant 3d orbitals.

Hybridisation:

$d^2sp^3$

The complex is diamagnetic.

### 6.4 Outer Orbital Complexes

Outer orbital complexes use outer nd orbitals for hybridisation.

For coordination number 6:

Hybridisation:

$sp^3d^2$

Geometry:

Octahedral.

These are generally high-spin complexes.

Example:

$[CoF_6]^{3-}$

Cobalt:

$Co^{3+}:3d^6$

$F^-$ is a weak-field ligand, so pairing does not occur in the 3d orbitals before hybridisation.

Hybridisation:

$sp^3d^2$

The complex has four unpaired electrons and is paramagnetic.

## 7. Crystal Field Theory (CFT)

### 7.1 Introduction

Crystal Field Theory explains the bonding and properties of coordination compounds by considering the electrostatic interaction between the central metal ion and ligands.

According to CFT:

- Ligands are treated as point charges or dipoles.
    
- The d-orbitals of the free metal ion initially have equal energy.
    
- When ligands approach the metal ion, electrostatic repulsion causes splitting of d-orbital energies.
    

This splitting is called crystal field splitting.

### 7.2 Degenerate d-Orbitals

In a free gaseous metal ion, all five d-orbitals have the same energy.

They are called degenerate orbitals.

The five d-orbitals are:

1. $d_{xy}$
    
2. $d_{yz}$
    
3. $d_{xz}$
    
4. $d_{x^2-y^2}$
    
5. $d_{z^2}$
    

### 7.3 Crystal Field Splitting in Octahedral Complexes

In an octahedral complex, six ligands approach the central metal ion along the x, y and z axes.

The orbitals pointing directly towards the ligands experience greater repulsion.

Therefore, the d-orbitals split into two groups.

#### Higher Energy Orbitals: $e_g$

- $d_{x^2-y^2}$
    
- $d_{z^2}$
    

These orbitals point directly towards the ligands.

#### Lower Energy Orbitals: $t_{2g}$

- $d_{xy}$
    
- $d_{yz}$
    
- $d_{xz}$
    

These orbitals lie between the axes.

The energy difference between these groups is called octahedral crystal field splitting energy.

$\Delta_o$

### 7.4 Energy of Orbitals in an Octahedral Field

Relative to the barycentre:

- Each $t_{2g}$ orbital has energy $-0.4\Delta_o$.
    
- Each $e_g$ orbital has energy $+0.6\Delta_o$.
    

Total energy remains unchanged:

$3(-0.4\Delta_o)+2(+0.6\Delta_o)=0$

### 7.5 Crystal Field Splitting in Tetrahedral Complexes

In tetrahedral complexes, four ligands approach the metal ion between the coordinate axes.

The splitting pattern is opposite to that of an octahedral complex.

#### Lower Energy Orbitals: $e$

- $d_{x^2-y^2}$
    
- $d_{z^2}$
    

#### Higher Energy Orbitals: $t_2$

- $d_{xy}$
    
- $d_{yz}$
    
- $d_{xz}$
    

### 7.6 Energy of Orbitals in a Tetrahedral Field

Relative to the barycentre:

- Each $e$ orbital has energy $-0.6\Delta_t$.
    
- Each $t_2$ orbital has energy $+0.4\Delta_t$.
    

The tetrahedral splitting is smaller than the octahedral splitting.

$\Delta_t=\dfrac{4}{9}\Delta_o$

### 7.7 Why Is Tetrahedral Splitting Smaller?

- Tetrahedral complexes have only four ligands.
    
- The ligands do not approach directly along the coordinate axes.
    
- The overall repulsion between ligands and d-electrons is smaller.
    

Therefore:

$\Delta_t<\Delta_o$

### 7.8 Square Planar Splitting

In square planar complexes, four ligands approach the central metal ion along the x and y axes.

The typical order of d-orbital energies from highest to lowest is:

$d_{x^2-y^2}>d_{xy}>d_{z^2}>d_{xz}=d_{yz}$

The exact splitting depends on the metal ion and ligands.

Square planar complexes are commonly associated with $d^8$ metal ions.

Example:

$[Ni(CN)_4]^{2-}$

### 7.11 High-Spin and Low-Spin Complexes

The distribution of electrons depends on the competition between:

- Crystal field splitting energy ($\Delta$).
    
- Pairing energy ($P$).
    

#### High-Spin Complexes

When:

$\Delta_o<P$

Electrons prefer to occupy higher-energy orbitals rather than pair up in lower-energy orbitals.

Properties:

- More unpaired electrons.
    
- Usually paramagnetic.
    
- Common with weak-field ligands.
    

#### Low-Spin Complexes

When:

$\Delta_o>P$

Electrons prefer to pair up in lower-energy orbitals.

Properties:

- Fewer unpaired electrons.
    
- May be diamagnetic or paramagnetic.
    
- Common with strong-field ligands.

$t_{2g}^4e_g^2$

Four unpaired electrons.

**Strong-field case:**

$t_{2g}^6e_g^0$

### 7.13 Crystal Field Stabilisation Energy (CFSE)

CFSE is the lowering in energy of d-electrons due to crystal field splitting relative to the barycentre.

For an octahedral complex:

$CFSE=(-0.4n_{t_{2g}}+0.6n_{e_g})\Delta_{o}+xP.E$


Where:

- $n_{t_{2g}}$ = number of electrons in lower orbitals.
    
- $n_{e_g}$ = number of electrons in higher orbitals.
    
- x= no of extra electron pair formed
#### Examples

**$d^1$ configuration**

$t_{2g}^1$

$CFSE=-0.4\Delta_o$

**$d^2$ configuration**

$t_{2g}^2$

$CFSE=-0.8\Delta_o$

**$d^3$ configuration**

$t_{2g}^3$

$CFSE=-1.2\Delta_o$

**$d^6$ low-spin configuration**

$t_{2g}^6$

$CFSE=-2.4\Delta_o$

**$d^6$ high-spin configuration**

$t_{2g}^4e_g^2$

$CFSE=-0.4\Delta_o$

Pairing energy must also be considered when comparing configurations.
ex: for $d^4$ in SFL:
	CFSE= -$0.4*4 +1*PE$
### 7.14 CFSE in Tetrahedral Complexes

For tetrahedral complexes:

$CFSE=(-0.6n_e+0.4n_{t_2})\Delta_t$

Where:

- $n_e$ = electrons in lower-energy orbitals.
    
- $n_{t_2}$ = electrons in higher-energy orbitals.
    

Most tetrahedral complexes are high spin because $\Delta_t$ is generally smaller than pairing energy.

## 8. Magnetic Properties of Coordination Compounds

### 8.1 Paramagnetic Complexes

Complexes containing one or more unpaired electrons are paramagnetic.

They are attracted by an external magnetic field.

Examples:

- $[FeF_6]^{3-}$
    
- $[CoF_6]^{3-}$
    
- $[NiCl_4]^{2-}$
    

### 8.2 Diamagnetic Complexes

Complexes in which all electrons are paired are diamagnetic.

They are weakly repelled by an external magnetic field.

Examples:

- $[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}$
    
- $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$
    
- $[Ni(CN)_4]^{2-}$
    

### 8.3 Magnetic Moment

The spin-only magnetic moment is calculated using:

$\mu=\sqrt{n(n+2)}\ BM$

Where $n$ is the number of unpaired electrons.

## 9. Colour of Coordination Compounds

### 9.1 Why Are Coordination Compounds Coloured?

Many coordination compounds are coloured because of electronic transitions between split d-orbitals.

When white light falls on a complex:

- Certain wavelengths are absorbed.
    
- Electrons gain energy and move to higher-energy d-orbitals.
    
- The remaining transmitted or reflected light produces the observed colour.
    

This is called a d–d transition.

### 9.2 Relation Between Colour and Crystal Field Splitting

The energy absorbed is:

$\Delta= h\nu$

Since:

$\nu=\dfrac{c}{\lambda}$

Therefore:

$\Delta=\dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$

Hence:

- Larger splitting means higher absorbed frequency.
    
- Larger splitting means shorter absorbed wavelength.
    

### 9.3 Why Are $d^0$ and $d^{10}$ Complexes Usually Colourless?

$d^0$ complexes:

There are no d-electrons available for ordinary d–d transitions.

$d^{10}$ complexes:

All d-orbitals are completely filled, so ordinary d–d transitions are not possible.

Examples:

- $Sc^{3+}$: $d^0$
    
- $Ti^{4+}$: $d^0$
    
- $Zn^{2+}$: $d^{10}$
    
- $Cu^+$: $d^{10}$
    

Some compounds can still show colour due to charge-transfer transitions.(ex: permanganate and dichromate)

### 9.4 Charge Transfer Transitions

In certain complexes, electrons move between ligand and metal orbitals.

These are called charge-transfer transitions.

They can produce intense colours even when ordinary d–d transitions are not possible.

## 10. Stability of Coordination Compounds

### 10.1 Stability Constant

The stability of a coordination complex is expressed using its formation or stability constant.

For:

$M+nL\rightleftharpoons ML_n$

The formation constant is:

$\beta_n=\dfrac{[ML_n]}{[M][L]^n}$

A higher value of $\beta_n$ indicates greater thermodynamic stability under the specified conditions.

### 10.2 Stepwise Formation Constants

Complex formation may occur in multiple steps.

$M+L\rightleftharpoons ML$

$ML+L\rightleftharpoons ML_2$

The stepwise stability constants are:

$K_1=\dfrac{[ML]}{[M][L]}$

$K_2=\dfrac{[ML_2]}{[ML][L]}$

Overall stability constant:

$\beta_2=K_1K_2$

### 10.3 Chelate Effect

Chelating ligands generally form more stable complexes than comparable complexes containing equivalent monodentate ligands.

This increased stability is called the chelate effect.

Examples:

- $[Cu(en)_2]^{2+}$
    
- $[Co(en)_3]^{3+}$
    

**Reason:** Chelation often produces a favourable entropy change because multiple monodentate ligands can be replaced by fewer multidentate ligands.

### 10.4 Factors Affecting Stability

- Charge on the central metal ion.
	    direct relation
- Size of the central metal ion.
	    inverse relation
- Strength of the ligand.
	    direct relation
- Denticity of the ligand.
	    direct relation
- Crystal field stabilisation.
	    direct relation
- Chelate formation.
	    direct relation to no of chelate rings
- Steric effects.
	    inverse relation
