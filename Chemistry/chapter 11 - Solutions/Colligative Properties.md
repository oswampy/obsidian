
Properties of dilute solutions that depend only on the **number of solute particles**, not their chemical nature, are called:

**Colligative properties**

There are four major colligative properties:

- Relative lowering of vapour pressure
    
- Elevation of boiling point
    
- Depression of freezing point
    
- Osmotic pressure
    

---

# Why Are They Called Colligative?

The word comes from the idea of particles being "collected together."

The important factor is:

$\boxed{\text{number of solute particles}}$

not:

$\boxed{\text{identity of solute}}$

For example, one mole of glucose and one mole of urea produce approximately the same colligative effect under ideal dilute conditions because both give approximately one mole of solute particles.

---
# Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure

For a solution containing a non-volatile solute:

$\Delta p=p_1^0-p_1$

Using Raoult's law:

$p_1=x_1p_1^0$

Therefore:

$\Delta p=p_1^0-x_1p_1^0$

$\Delta p=p_1^0(1-x_1)$

Since:

$x_1+x_2=1$

we get:

$\Delta p=p_1^0x_2$

Therefore:

$\boxed{\frac{\Delta p}{p_1^0}=x_2}$

This is the **relative lowering of vapour pressure**.

---

# Elevation of Boiling Point

Boiling occurs when:

$\text{Vapour pressure}=\text{External pressure}$

Adding a non-volatile solute lowers the vapour pressure.

Therefore a higher temperature is required to make vapour pressure equal to atmospheric pressure.

Hence:

$\boxed{\text{Boiling point increases}}$

---

## Formula for Elevation of Boiling Point

Let:

$T_b^0$ = boiling point of pure solvent

$T_b$ = boiling point of solution

Then:

$\Delta T_b=T_b-T_b^0$

For a dilute solution:

$\boxed{\Delta T_b=K_bm}$

where:

$K_b$ = molal elevation constant

$m$ = molality

---

## Molal Elevation Constant

$K_b$ is the elevation in boiling point produced when:

$\boxed{1\text{ mol solute is dissolved in }1\text{ kg solvent}}$

provided the solution behaves ideally and is sufficiently dilute.

---

## Formula Using Masses

$m=\frac{w_2/M_2}{w_1/1000}$

Therefore:

$m=\frac{1000w_2}{M_2w_1}$

Hence:

$\boxed{\Delta T_b=K_b\frac{1000w_2}{M_2w_1}}$

Therefore molar mass:

$\boxed{M_2=\frac{K_b1000w_2}{\Delta T_bw_1}}$

This is a **very important numerical formula**.

---

# Depression in Freezing Point

The freezing point of a solution is lower than that of the pure solvent.

Therefore:

$\boxed{\Delta T_f=T_f^0-T_f}$

For dilute solutions:

$\boxed{\Delta T_f=K_fm}$

where:

$K_f$ = molal depression constant.

---

## Why Does Freezing Point Decrease?

The presence of solute lowers the chemical potential of the liquid solvent.

Therefore the liquid must be cooled to a lower temperature before it reaches equilibrium with the solid phase.

Hence:

$\boxed{T_f\downarrow}$

---

## Formula Using Masses

$m=\frac{1000w_2}{M_2w_1}$

Therefore:

$\boxed{\Delta T_f=K_f\frac{1000w_2}{M_2w_1}}$

Thus:

$\boxed{M_2=\frac{K_f1000w_2}{\Delta T_fw_1}}$

---

### Applications of Depression in Freezing Point

### Antifreeze

Ethylene glycol is added to water in automobile cooling systems.

It lowers the freezing point.

### Salt on icy roads

Adding salt lowers the freezing point of water, helping melt ice.

---

# Osmosis

The spontaneous movement of solvent molecules through a semipermeable membrane from pure solvent or dilute solution to concentrated solution is called:

**Osmosis**

The membrane allows solvent molecules to pass but prevents solute particles from passing.

---

## Semipermeable Membrane

A semipermeable membrane permits only certain particles, usually solvent molecules, to pass through.

Examples:

- Animal membranes
    
- Cellophane
    
- Copper ferrocyanide membrane
    

---

## Osmotic Pressure

The minimum pressure that must be applied to the solution to stop osmosis is called:

**Osmotic pressure**

It is represented by:

$\pi$

For dilute solutions:

$\boxed{\pi=CRT}$

where:

- $\pi$ = osmotic pressure
    
- $C$ = molar concentration
    
- $R$ = gas constant
    
- $T$ = temperature
    

Since:

$C=\frac{n}{V}$

we get:

$\boxed{\pi=\frac{n}{V}RT}$

---

## Osmotic Pressure in Terms of Mass

Since:

$n=\frac{w_2}{M_2}$

we get:

$\pi=\frac{w_2}{M_2V}RT$

Therefore:

$\boxed{M_2=\frac{w_2RT}{\pi V}}$

This is one of the most important formulas in the chapter.

---

## Why Osmotic Pressure is Important

Osmotic pressure is particularly useful for finding molar masses of:

- Proteins
    
- Polymers
    
- Biological macromolecules
    

because it can be measured at room temperature and uses very dilute solutions.

---

## Reverse Osmosis

If pressure greater than the osmotic pressure is applied to the solution side:

$\boxed{P>\pi}$

solvent flows from the solution towards the pure solvent side.

This process is called:

**Reverse osmosis**

---

## Application of Reverse Osmosis

Reverse osmosis is used for:

**Desalination of seawater**

High pressure is applied to seawater, forcing water through a semipermeable membrane while many dissolved salts are retained.

---

# Isotonic Solutions

Two solutions having the same osmotic pressure at the same temperature are called:

**Isotonic solutions**

Therefore:

$\pi_1=\pi_2$

For dilute solutions:

$C_1RT=C_2RT$

Therefore:

$\boxed{C_1=C_2}$

assuming the same temperature and ideal behaviour.

---

# Hypertonic and Hypotonic

### Hypertonic solution

Has greater osmotic pressure.

$\pi_{hyper}>\pi_{hypo}$

Water tends to move towards the hypertonic solution.

### Hypotonic solution

Has lower osmotic pressure.

Water tends to move out of the hypotonic side towards the hypertonic side.

---

# Colligative Properties Summary

|Property|Formula|
|---|---|
|Relative lowering of vapour pressure|$\frac{\Delta p}{p^0}=x_2$|
|Elevation of boiling point|$\Delta T_b=K_bm$|
|Depression of freezing point|$\Delta T_f=K_fm$|
|Osmotic pressure|$\pi=CRT$|
