

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of two or more components.

The component present in larger amount is generally called the **solvent**, while the component present in smaller amount is called the **solute**.

For example, in a salt-water solution:

- Water → solvent
    
- NaCl → solute
    

A solution can exist in any physical state, but Class 12 mainly deals with **liquid solutions**.

---

# Types of Solutions

Solutions can be classified according to the physical states of their components.

|Solute|Solvent|Example|
|---|---|---|
|Gas|Gas|Air|
|Gas|Liquid|CO₂ in water|
|Liquid|Liquid|Ethanol in water|
|Solid|Liquid|NaCl in water|
|Solid|Solid|Alloys|

---

# Binary Solutions

A solution containing only two components is called a **binary solution**.

For example:

$\text{solute}+\text{solvent}=\text{binary solution}$

Most mathematical treatment in this chapter deals with binary solutions.

---

# Concentration of Solutions

Concentration tells us the amount of solute present in a given amount of solution or solvent.

Important ways of expressing concentration:

- Mass percentage
    
- Volume percentage
    
- Mass by volume percentage
    
- Parts per million
    
- Mole fraction
    
- Molarity
    
- Molality
    

---

# Mass Percentage

Mass percentage is:

$\text{Mass %}=\frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solution}}\times100$

Since:

$\text{Mass of solution}=\text{Mass of solute}+\text{Mass of solvent}$

we can also write:

$\text{Mass \%}=\frac{w_2}{w_1+w_2}\times100$

where:

- $w_1$ = mass of solvent
    
- $w_2$ = mass of solute
    

---

# Volume Percentage

Used mainly when both components are liquids.

$\text{Volume\%}=\frac{\text{Volume of solute}}{\text{Volume of solution}}\times100$

Example:

A $10%$ ethanol solution by volume means:

$10\text{ mL ethanol}$ is present in every $100\text{ mL solution}$.

---

# Mass by Volume Percentage

$\text{Mass/Volume %}=\frac{\text{Mass of solute in g}}{\text{Volume of solution in mL}}\times100$

Example:

A $5%$ glucose solution means:

$5\text{ g}$ glucose is present in $100\text{ mL}$ solution.

---

# Parts Per Million

Used when the amount of solute is extremely small.

$\text{ppm}=\frac{\text{Number of parts of component}}{\text{Total number of parts of solution}}\times10^6$

For mass concentrations:

$\text{ppm}=\frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solution}}\times10^6$

For very dilute aqueous solutions:

$1\text{ ppm}\approx1\text{ mg/L}$

---

# Mole Fraction

Mole fraction is the ratio of the number of moles of a component to the total number of moles.

For a binary solution:

$x_1=\frac{n_1}{n_1+n_2}$

$x_2=\frac{n_2}{n_1+n_2}$

Therefore:

$x_1+x_2=1$

For solute:

$x_2=\frac{n_2}{n_1+n_2}$

For solvent:

$x_1=\frac{n_1}{n_1+n_2}$

---


# Molarity

Molarity is the number of moles of solute present per litre of **solution**.

$M=\frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{volume of solution in L}}$

Therefore:

$M=\frac{n}{V}$

If $w$ grams of solute having molar mass $M_m$ are dissolved to make $V$ L solution:

$M=\frac{w}{M_mV}$

### Unit

$\text{mol L}^{-1}$

or simply:

$\boxed{M}$

---

# Molality

Molality is the number of moles of solute present per kilogram of **solvent**.

$m=\frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{mass of solvent in kg}}$

If $w_2$ g solute is dissolved in $w_1$ g solvent:

$m=\frac{w_2/M_2}{w_1/1000}$

Therefore:

$\boxed{m=\frac{1000w_2}{M_2w_1}}$

### Unit

$\text{mol kg}^{-1}$

---

# Molarity vs Molality

|Molarity|Molality|
|---|---|
|Moles per litre of solution|Moles per kg of solvent|
|$M=\frac{n}{V_{solution}}$|$m=\frac{n}{W_{solvent}}$|
|Depends on temperature|Independent of temperature|
|Volume changes with temperature|Mass does not change with temperature|

### JEE favourite

**Molality is preferred in colligative-property calculations** because it is temperature independent.

---

# Relation Between Molarity and Molality

Suppose:

- Density of solution = $d$ g mL⁻¹
    
- Molarity = $M$
    
- Molar mass of solute = $M_2$
    

Then:

$\boxed{m=\frac{1000M}{1000d-MM_2}}$

Similarly:

$\boxed{M=\frac{1000dm}{1000+mM_2}}$

These are very useful JEE formulas.

---

# Solubility

Solubility is the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a particular temperature.

It depends on:

- Nature of solute
    
- Nature of solvent
    
- Temperature
    
- Pressure, particularly for gases
    

---

# Solubility of Solids in Liquids

Generally, solubility of a solid in a liquid increases with temperature.

However, this is **not universal**.

It depends on the enthalpy change of dissolution.

If dissolution is endothermic:

$\Delta H_{sol}>0$

increasing temperature generally increases solubility.

If dissolution is exothermic:

$\Delta H_{sol}<0$

increasing temperature may decrease solubility.

---

# Solubility of Gases in Liquids

The solubility of gases depends strongly on:

- Pressure
    
- Temperature
    
- Nature of gas and solvent
    

---

# Effect of Temperature on Gas Solubility

Generally:

$\boxed{\text{Gas solubility decreases as temperature increases}}$

This is because dissolution of gases in liquids is generally exothermic.

Example:

Carbonated drinks lose dissolved $CO_2$ more rapidly when warm.

---

# Effect of Pressure on Gas Solubility

The solubility of a gas in a liquid increases with pressure.

This is given quantitatively by **Henry's law**.

---

# Henry's Law

At constant temperature:

$p=K_Hx$

where:

- $p$ = partial pressure of gas
    
- $x$ = mole fraction of gas dissolved in solution
    
- $K_H$ = Henry's law constant
    

Therefore:

$x=\frac{p}{K_H}$

---

# Important Henry's Law Result

From:

$p=K_Hx$

we get:

$\boxed{x\propto p}$

Therefore:

**Higher pressure → greater gas solubility**

---

# Effect of $K_H$

Since:

$x=\frac{p}{K_H}$

for the same pressure:

$\boxed{\text{Higher }K_H\rightarrow\text{lower solubility}}$

and:

$\boxed{\text{Lower }K_H\rightarrow\text{higher solubility}}$



---

# Applications of Henry's Law

### Carbonated beverages

$CO_2$ is dissolved under high pressure.

When the bottle is opened:

$\text{Pressure}\downarrow$

Therefore:

$\text{Solubility of }CO_2\downarrow$

and $CO_2$ escapes.

### Scuba diving

At high pressure, more gases dissolve in blood.

Rapid ascent can cause dissolved gases to come out of solution, leading to **decompression sickness**.

### High altitudes

At high altitude, atmospheric pressure is lower.

Therefore the amount of oxygen dissolved in blood decreases.

This can contribute to **anoxia**.

---

# Vapour Pressure

A liquid in a closed container establishes equilibrium:

$\text{Liquid}\rightleftharpoons\text{Vapour}$

The pressure exerted by the vapour at equilibrium is called:

**Vapour pressure**

- it does not depend on shape/size of container
- it depends on nature of substance
- inversely proportional to force of attraction

---

# Raoult's Law

For a solution of volatile liquids:

$p_1=x_1p_1^0$

and:

$p_2=x_2p_2^0$

where:

- $p_1,p_2$ = partial vapour pressures
    
- $p_1^0,p_2^0$ = vapour pressures of pure components
    
- $x_1,x_2$ = mole fractions
    

Total vapour pressure:

$p=p_1+p_2$

Therefore:

$\boxed{p=x_1p_1^0+x_2p_2^0}$

---

# Derivation of Raoult's Law for Binary Solution

Since:

$p_1=x_1p_1^0$

and:

$p_2=x_2p_2^0$

Total pressure is:

$p=p_1+p_2$

Therefore:

$p=x_1p_1^0+x_2p_2^0$

Since:

$x_1=1-x_2$

we can write:

$p=(1-x_2)p_1^0+x_2p_2^0$

or:

$\boxed{p=p_1^0+x_2(p_2^0-p_1^0)}$

This is the equation of a straight line.

---

# Vapour Pressure vs Mole Fraction

For an ideal binary solution, partial vapour pressures vary linearly with mole fraction.

At:

$x_1=1$

$p_1=p_1^0$

At:

$x_1=0$

$p_1=0$

---

# Non-Volatile Solute in a Volatile Solvent

Suppose a non-volatile solute is dissolved in a volatile solvent.

The solute contributes essentially no vapour pressure.

Therefore:

$p=x_1p_1^0$

Since:

$x_1<1$

we get:

$p<p_1^0$

Therefore:

\boxed{\text{Addition of non-volatile solute lowers vapour pressure}}

---

# Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure

For a solution containing a non-volatile solute:

$\Delta p=p_1^0-p_1$

Using Raoult's law:

$p_1=x_1p_1^0$

Therefore:

$\Delta p=p_1^0-x_1p_1^0$

$\Delta p=p_1^0(1-x_1)$

Since:

$x_1+x_2=1$

we get:

$\Delta p=p_1^0x_2$

Therefore:

$\boxed{\frac{\Delta p}{p_1^0}=x_2}$

This is the **relative lowering of vapour pressure**.

---

# In Terms of Number of Moles

$x_2=\frac{n_2}{n_1+n_2}$

For a dilute solution:

$n_2\ll n_1$

Therefore:

$x_2\approx\frac{n_2}{n_1}$

Hence:

$\boxed{\frac{\Delta p}{p_1^0}=\frac{n_2}{n_1}}$

---

# Ideal Solutions

A solution that obeys **Raoult's law over the entire range of concentration** is called an ideal solution.

For an ideal solution:

$p_1=x_1p_1^0$

$p_2=x_2p_2^0$

---

# Conditions for an Ideal Solution

For an ideal solution:

$\Delta H_{mix}=0$

and:

$\Delta V_{mix}=0$

The intermolecular interactions should be approximately similar:

$A-A\approx B-B\approx A-B$

Examples:

- Benzene + Toluene
    
- $n$-hexane + $n$-heptane
    

---

# Non-Ideal Solutions

Solutions that do not obey Raoult's law are called **non-ideal solutions**.

They show:

- Positive deviation
    
- Negative deviation
    

---

# Positive Deviation from Raoult's Law

In positive deviation:

$p_{observed}>p_{Raoult}$

The $A-B$ interactions are **weaker** than the $A-A$ and $B-B$ interactions.

Therefore molecules escape more easily into the vapour phase.

Hence:

$\boxed{\Delta H_{mix}>0}$

and generally:

$\boxed{\Delta V_{mix}>0}$

### Result

Vapour pressure is higher than expected.

Boiling point is lower than expected.

---

# Examples of Positive Deviation

### Ethanol + Acetone

Acetone disrupts hydrogen bonding between ethanol molecules.

Therefore intermolecular attraction decreases.

### Carbon disulfide + Acetone

The interactions between unlike molecules are weaker.

---

# Negative Deviation from Raoult's Law

In negative deviation:

$p_{observed}<p_{Raoult}$

The $A-B$ interactions are **stronger** than the $A-A$ and $B-B$ interactions.

Therefore molecules are held more strongly in the liquid phase.

Hence:

$\boxed{\Delta H_{mix}<0}$

and generally:

$\boxed{\Delta V_{mix}<0}$

### Result

Vapour pressure is lower than expected.

Boiling point is higher than expected.

---

# Examples of Negative Deviation

### Chloroform + Acetone

Strong hydrogen bonding develops between:

$H$ of $CHCl_3$

and:

$O$ of acetone.

Therefore:

$A-B>A-A,B-B$

---

### Nitric Acid + Water

Strong interactions occur between $HNO_3$ and $H_2O$.

Hence negative deviation.

---

# Positive vs Negative Deviation

|Positive deviation|Negative deviation|
|---|---|
|$A-B$ weaker|$A-B$ stronger|
|Vapour pressure higher|Vapour pressure lower|
|$\Delta H_{mix}>0$|$\Delta H_{mix}<0$|
|$\Delta V_{mix}>0$ generally|$\Delta V_{mix}<0$ generally|
|Boiling point lower|Boiling point higher|
|Ethanol + acetone|Chloroform + acetone|

---

# Azeotropes

An azeotrope is a mixture that boils at a constant temperature and whose liquid and vapour compositions are the same.

Therefore:

$\boxed{x_{liquid}=x_{vapour}}$

Azeotropes cannot be separated by ordinary fractional distillation.

---

# Minimum Boiling Azeotrope

Produced by **positive deviation** from Raoult's law.

It has:

- Lower boiling point than either component
    
- Maximum vapour pressure
    

Example:

**Ethanol-water mixture**

---

# Maximum Boiling Azeotrope

Produced by **negative deviation** from Raoult's law.

It has:

- Higher boiling point than either component
    
- Minimum vapour pressure
    

Example:

**Nitric acid-water mixture**

---





# Abnormal Molar Mass

Sometimes experimentally determined molar mass differs from the expected molar mass.

This happens because solute particles may:

- Associate
    
- Dissociate
    

Therefore the actual number of particles differs from what we expect.

This causes **abnormal colligative properties**.

---



# Important Graph Concepts

## Ideal Solution

Partial vapour pressure vs mole fraction is linear.

Total vapour pressure lies between the vapour pressures of the pure components.

---

## Positive Deviation

The total vapour pressure curve lies **above** the ideal Raoult's-law line.

Therefore:

$\boxed{P_{actual}>P_{ideal}}$

---

## Negative Deviation

The total vapour pressure curve lies **below** the ideal Raoult's-law line.

Therefore:

$\boxed{P_{actual}<P_{ideal}}$

---

# Boiling Point and Vapour Pressure Relationship

At a fixed external pressure:

Higher vapour pressure at a given temperature means the liquid reaches the boiling condition sooner.

Therefore:

### Positive deviation

$\text{Vapour pressure}\uparrow$

$\text{Boiling point}\downarrow$

### Negative deviation

$\text{Vapour pressure}\downarrow$

$\text{Boiling point}\uparrow$

---

# Important Concept: Volatile Solute

If both components are volatile:

$p_1=x_1p_1^0$

$p_2=x_2p_2^0$

and:

$p_{total}=p_1+p_2$

Do **not** use:

$\frac{\Delta p}{p^0}=x_{solute}$

directly unless the solute is **non-volatile**.

---

# Important Concept: Dilute Solution Approximation

For dilute solutions:

$n_{solute}\ll n_{solvent}$

Therefore:

$n_{solvent}+n_{solute}\approx n_{solvent}$

Hence:

$x_{solute}\approx\frac{n_{solute}}{n_{solvent}}$

This approximation is used extensively in derivations.

---

#  Mole Fraction After Dissociation

Suppose $1$ mole of $NaCl$ is dissolved and dissociates completely:

$NaCl\rightarrow Na^++Cl^-$

The number of solute particles becomes:

$2$

For colligative properties, count the **actual particles**, not the original formula units.

This is exactly why the van't Hoff factor is required.
