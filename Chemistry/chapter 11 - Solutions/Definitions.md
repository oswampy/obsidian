
## Introduction

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of two or more components.

The component present in larger amount is generally called the **solvent**, while the component present in smaller amount is called the **solute**.

For example, in a salt-water solution:

- Water → solvent
    
- NaCl → solute
    

A solution can exist in any physical state, but Class 12 mainly deals with **liquid solutions**.

---

# Types of Solutions

Solutions can be classified according to the physical states of their components.

|Solute|Solvent|Example|
|---|---|---|
|Gas|Gas|Air|
|Gas|Liquid|CO₂ in water|
|Liquid|Liquid|Ethanol in water|
|Solid|Liquid|NaCl in water|
|Solid|Solid|Alloys|

---

# Binary Solutions

A solution containing only two components is called a **binary solution**.

For example:

$\text{solute}+\text{solvent}=\text{binary solution}$

Most mathematical treatment in this chapter deals with binary solutions.

---

# Concentration of Solutions

Concentration tells us the amount of solute present in a given amount of solution or solvent.

Important ways of expressing concentration:

- Mass percentage
    
- Volume percentage
    
- Mass by volume percentage
    
- Parts per million
    
- Mole fraction
    
- Molarity
    
- Molality
    

---

# Mass Percentage

Mass percentage is:

$\text{Mass %}=\frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solution}}\times100$

Since:

$\text{Mass of solution}=\text{Mass of solute}+\text{Mass of solvent}$

we can also write:

$\text{Mass \%}=\frac{w_2}{w_1+w_2}\times100$

where:

- $w_1$ = mass of solvent
    
- $w_2$ = mass of solute
    

---

# Volume Percentage

Used mainly when both components are liquids.

$\text{Volume\%}=\frac{\text{Volume of solute}}{\text{Volume of solution}}\times100$

Example:

A $10%$ ethanol solution by volume means:

$10\text{ mL ethanol}$ is present in every $100\text{ mL solution}$.

---

# Mass by Volume Percentage

$\text{Mass/Volume %}=\frac{\text{Mass of solute in g}}{\text{Volume of solution in mL}}\times100$

Example:

A $5%$ glucose solution means:

$5\text{ g}$ glucose is present in $100\text{ mL}$ solution.

---

# Parts Per Million

Used when the amount of solute is extremely small.

$\text{ppm}=\frac{\text{Number of parts of component}}{\text{Total number of parts of solution}}\times10^6$

For mass concentrations:

$\text{ppm}=\frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solution}}\times10^6$

For very dilute aqueous solutions:

$1\text{ ppm}\approx1\text{ mg/L}$

---

# Mole Fraction

Mole fraction is the ratio of the number of moles of a component to the total number of moles.

For a binary solution:

$x_1=\frac{n_1}{n_1+n_2}$

$x_2=\frac{n_2}{n_1+n_2}$

Therefore:

$x_1+x_2=1$

For solute:

$x_2=\frac{n_2}{n_1+n_2}$

For solvent:

$x_1=\frac{n_1}{n_1+n_2}$

---


# Molarity

Molarity is the number of moles of solute present per litre of **solution**.

$M=\frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{volume of solution in L}}$

Therefore:

$M=\frac{n}{V}$

If $w$ grams of solute having molar mass $M_m$ are dissolved to make $V$ L solution:

$M=\frac{w}{M_mV}$

### Unit

$\text{mol L}^{-1}$

or simply:

$\boxed{M}$

---

# Molality

Molality is the number of moles of solute present per kilogram of **solvent**.

$m=\frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{mass of solvent in kg}}$

If $w_2$ g solute is dissolved in $w_1$ g solvent:

$m=\frac{w_2/M_2}{w_1/1000}$

Therefore:

$\boxed{m=\frac{1000w_2}{M_2w_1}}$

### Unit

$\text{mol kg}^{-1}$

---

# Molarity vs Molality

|Molarity|Molality|
|---|---|
|Moles per litre of solution|Moles per kg of solvent|
|$M=\frac{n}{V_{solution}}$|$m=\frac{n}{W_{solvent}}$|
|Depends on temperature|Independent of temperature|
|Volume changes with temperature|Mass does not change with temperature|

### JEE favourite

**Molality is preferred in colligative-property calculations** because it is temperature independent.

---

# Relation Between Molarity and Molality

Suppose:

- Density of solution = $d$ g mL⁻¹
    
- Molarity = $M$
    
- Molar mass of solute = $M_2$
    

Then:

$\boxed{m=\frac{1000M}{1000d-MM_2}}$

Similarly:

$\boxed{M=\frac{1000dm}{1000+mM_2}}$

These are very useful JEE formulas.

---

# Solubility

Solubility is the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a particular temperature.

It depends on:

- Nature of solute
    
- Nature of solvent
    
- Temperature
    
- Pressure, particularly for gases
    

---

# Solubility of Solids in Liquids

Generally, solubility of a solid in a liquid increases with temperature.

However, this is **not universal**.

It depends on the enthalpy change of dissolution.

If dissolution is endothermic:

$\Delta H_{sol}>0$

increasing temperature generally increases solubility.

If dissolution is exothermic:

$\Delta H_{sol}<0$

increasing temperature may decrease solubility.

---

# Solubility of Gases in Liquids

The solubility of gases depends strongly on:

- Pressure
    
- Temperature
    
- Nature of gas and solvent
    

---

# Effect of Temperature on Gas Solubility

Generally:

$\boxed{\text{Gas solubility decreases as temperature increases}}$

This is because dissolution of gases in liquids is generally exothermic.

Example:

Carbonated drinks lose dissolved $CO_2$ more rapidly when warm.

---

# Effect of Pressure on Gas Solubility

The solubility of a gas in a liquid increases with pressure.

This is given quantitatively by **Henry's law**.

---

# Henry's Law

At constant temperature:

$p=K_Hx$

where:

- $p$ = partial pressure of gas
    
- $x$ = mole fraction of gas dissolved in solution
    
- $K_H$ = Henry's law constant
    

Therefore:

$x=\frac{p}{K_H}$

---

# Important Henry's Law Result

From:

$p=K_Hx$

we get:

$\boxed{x\propto p}$

Therefore:

**Higher pressure → greater gas solubility**

---

# Effect of $K_H$

Since:

$x=\frac{p}{K_H}$

for the same pressure:

$\boxed{\text{Higher }K_H\rightarrow\text{lower solubility}}$

and:

$\boxed{\text{Lower }K_H\rightarrow\text{higher solubility}}$



---

# Applications of Henry's Law

### Carbonated beverages

$CO_2$ is dissolved under high pressure.

When the bottle is opened:

$\text{Pressure}\downarrow$

Therefore:

$\text{Solubility of }CO_2\downarrow$

and $CO_2$ escapes.

### Scuba diving

At high pressure, more gases dissolve in blood.

Rapid ascent can cause dissolved gases to come out of solution, leading to **decompression sickness**.

### High altitudes

At high altitude, atmospheric pressure is lower.

Therefore the amount of oxygen dissolved in blood decreases.

This can contribute to **anoxia**.

---

# Vapour Pressure

A liquid in a closed container establishes equilibrium:

$\text{Liquid}\rightleftharpoons\text{Vapour}$

The pressure exerted by the vapour at equilibrium is called:

**Vapour pressure**

---

# Raoult's Law

For a solution of volatile liquids:

$p_1=x_1p_1^0$

and:

$p_2=x_2p_2^0$

where:

- $p_1,p_2$ = partial vapour pressures
    
- $p_1^0,p_2^0$ = vapour pressures of pure components
    
- $x_1,x_2$ = mole fractions
    

Total vapour pressure:

$p=p_1+p_2$

Therefore:

$\boxed{p=x_1p_1^0+x_2p_2^0}$

---

# Derivation of Raoult's Law for Binary Solution

Since:

$p_1=x_1p_1^0$

and:

$p_2=x_2p_2^0$

Total pressure is:

$p=p_1+p_2$

Therefore:

$p=x_1p_1^0+x_2p_2^0$

Since:

$x_1=1-x_2$

we can write:

$p=(1-x_2)p_1^0+x_2p_2^0$

or:

$\boxed{p=p_1^0+x_2(p_2^0-p_1^0)}$

This is the equation of a straight line.

---

# Vapour Pressure vs Mole Fraction

For an ideal binary solution, partial vapour pressures vary linearly with mole fraction.

At:

$x_1=1$

$p_1=p_1^0$

At:

$x_1=0$

$p_1=0$

---

# Non-Volatile Solute in a Volatile Solvent

Suppose a non-volatile solute is dissolved in a volatile solvent.

The solute contributes essentially no vapour pressure.

Therefore:

$p=x_1p_1^0$

Since:

$x_1<1$

we get:

$p<p_1^0$

Therefore:

\boxed{\text{Addition of non-volatile solute lowers vapour pressure}}

---

# Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure

For a solution containing a non-volatile solute:

$\Delta p=p_1^0-p_1$

Using Raoult's law:

$p_1=x_1p_1^0$

Therefore:

$\Delta p=p_1^0-x_1p_1^0$

$\Delta p=p_1^0(1-x_1)$

Since:

$x_1+x_2=1$

we get:

$\Delta p=p_1^0x_2$

Therefore:

$\boxed{\frac{\Delta p}{p_1^0}=x_2}$

This is the **relative lowering of vapour pressure**.

---

# In Terms of Number of Moles

$x_2=\frac{n_2}{n_1+n_2}$

For a dilute solution:

$n_2\ll n_1$

Therefore:

$x_2\approx\frac{n_2}{n_1}$

Hence:

$\boxed{\frac{\Delta p}{p_1^0}=\frac{n_2}{n_1}}$

---

# Ideal Solutions

A solution that obeys **Raoult's law over the entire range of concentration** is called an ideal solution.

For an ideal solution:

$p_1=x_1p_1^0$

$p_2=x_2p_2^0$

---

# Conditions for an Ideal Solution

For an ideal solution:

$\Delta H_{mix}=0$

and:

$\Delta V_{mix}=0$

The intermolecular interactions should be approximately similar:

$A-A\approx B-B\approx A-B$

Examples:

- Benzene + Toluene
    
- $n$-hexane + $n$-heptane
    

---

# Non-Ideal Solutions

Solutions that do not obey Raoult's law are called **non-ideal solutions**.

They show:

- Positive deviation
    
- Negative deviation
    

---

# Positive Deviation from Raoult's Law

In positive deviation:

$p_{observed}>p_{Raoult}$

The $A-B$ interactions are **weaker** than the $A-A$ and $B-B$ interactions.

Therefore molecules escape more easily into the vapour phase.

Hence:

$\boxed{\Delta H_{mix}>0}$

and generally:

$\boxed{\Delta V_{mix}>0}$

### Result

Vapour pressure is higher than expected.

Boiling point is lower than expected.

---

# Examples of Positive Deviation

### Ethanol + Acetone

Acetone disrupts hydrogen bonding between ethanol molecules.

Therefore intermolecular attraction decreases.

### Carbon disulfide + Acetone

The interactions between unlike molecules are weaker.

---

# Negative Deviation from Raoult's Law

In negative deviation:

$p_{observed}<p_{Raoult}$

The $A-B$ interactions are **stronger** than the $A-A$ and $B-B$ interactions.

Therefore molecules are held more strongly in the liquid phase.

Hence:

$\boxed{\Delta H_{mix}<0}$

and generally:

$\boxed{\Delta V_{mix}<0}$

### Result

Vapour pressure is lower than expected.

Boiling point is higher than expected.

---

# Examples of Negative Deviation

### Chloroform + Acetone

Strong hydrogen bonding develops between:

$H$ of $CHCl_3$

and:

$O$ of acetone.

Therefore:

$A-B>A-A,B-B$

---

### Nitric Acid + Water

Strong interactions occur between $HNO_3$ and $H_2O$.

Hence negative deviation.

---

# Positive vs Negative Deviation

|Positive deviation|Negative deviation|
|---|---|
|$A-B$ weaker|$A-B$ stronger|
|Vapour pressure higher|Vapour pressure lower|
|$\Delta H_{mix}>0$|$\Delta H_{mix}<0$|
|$\Delta V_{mix}>0$ generally|$\Delta V_{mix}<0$ generally|
|Boiling point lower|Boiling point higher|
|Ethanol + acetone|Chloroform + acetone|

---

# Azeotropes

An azeotrope is a mixture that boils at a constant temperature and whose liquid and vapour compositions are the same.

Therefore:

$\boxed{x_{liquid}=x_{vapour}}$

Azeotropes cannot be separated by ordinary fractional distillation.

---

# Minimum Boiling Azeotrope

Produced by **positive deviation** from Raoult's law.

It has:

- Lower boiling point than either component
    
- Maximum vapour pressure
    

Example:

**Ethanol-water mixture**

---

# Maximum Boiling Azeotrope

Produced by **negative deviation** from Raoult's law.

It has:

- Higher boiling point than either component
    
- Minimum vapour pressure
    

Example:

**Nitric acid-water mixture**

---

# Colligative Properties

Properties of dilute solutions that depend only on the **number of solute particles**, not their chemical nature, are called:

**Colligative properties**

There are four major colligative properties:

- Relative lowering of vapour pressure
    
- Elevation of boiling point
    
- Depression of freezing point
    
- Osmotic pressure
    

---

# Why Are They Called Colligative?

The word comes from the idea of particles being "collected together."

The important factor is:

$\boxed{\text{number of solute particles}}$

not:

$\boxed{\text{identity of solute}}$

For example, one mole of glucose and one mole of urea produce approximately the same colligative effect under ideal dilute conditions because both give approximately one mole of solute particles.

---

# Elevation of Boiling Point

Boiling occurs when:

$\text{Vapour pressure}=\text{External pressure}$

Adding a non-volatile solute lowers the vapour pressure.

Therefore a higher temperature is required to make vapour pressure equal to atmospheric pressure.

Hence:

$\boxed{\text{Boiling point increases}}$

---

# Formula for Elevation of Boiling Point

Let:

$T_b^0$ = boiling point of pure solvent

$T_b$ = boiling point of solution

Then:

$\Delta T_b=T_b-T_b^0$

For a dilute solution:

$\boxed{\Delta T_b=K_bm}$

where:

$K_b$ = molal elevation constant

$m$ = molality

---

# Molal Elevation Constant

$K_b$ is the elevation in boiling point produced when:

$\boxed{1\text{ mol solute is dissolved in }1\text{ kg solvent}}$

provided the solution behaves ideally and is sufficiently dilute.

---

# Formula Using Masses

$m=\frac{w_2/M_2}{w_1/1000}$

Therefore:

$m=\frac{1000w_2}{M_2w_1}$

Hence:

$\boxed{\Delta T_b=K_b\frac{1000w_2}{M_2w_1}}$

Therefore molar mass:

$\boxed{M_2=\frac{K_b1000w_2}{\Delta T_bw_1}}$

This is a **very important numerical formula**.

---

# Depression in Freezing Point

The freezing point of a solution is lower than that of the pure solvent.

Therefore:

$\boxed{\Delta T_f=T_f^0-T_f}$

For dilute solutions:

$\boxed{\Delta T_f=K_fm}$

where:

$K_f$ = molal depression constant.

---

# Why Does Freezing Point Decrease?

The presence of solute lowers the chemical potential of the liquid solvent.

Therefore the liquid must be cooled to a lower temperature before it reaches equilibrium with the solid phase.

Hence:

$\boxed{T_f\downarrow}$

---

# Formula Using Masses

$m=\frac{1000w_2}{M_2w_1}$

Therefore:

$\boxed{\Delta T_f=K_f\frac{1000w_2}{M_2w_1}}$

Thus:

$\boxed{M_2=\frac{K_f1000w_2}{\Delta T_fw_1}}$

---

# Applications of Depression in Freezing Point

### Antifreeze

Ethylene glycol is added to water in automobile cooling systems.

It lowers the freezing point.

### Salt on icy roads

Adding salt lowers the freezing point of water, helping melt ice.

---

# Osmosis

The spontaneous movement of solvent molecules through a semipermeable membrane from pure solvent or dilute solution to concentrated solution is called:

**Osmosis**

The membrane allows solvent molecules to pass but prevents solute particles from passing.

---

# Semipermeable Membrane

A semipermeable membrane permits only certain particles, usually solvent molecules, to pass through.

Examples:

- Animal membranes
    
- Cellophane
    
- Copper ferrocyanide membrane
    

---

# Osmotic Pressure

The minimum pressure that must be applied to the solution to stop osmosis is called:

**Osmotic pressure**

It is represented by:

$\pi$

For dilute solutions:

$\boxed{\pi=CRT}$

where:

- $\pi$ = osmotic pressure
    
- $C$ = molar concentration
    
- $R$ = gas constant
    
- $T$ = temperature
    

Since:

$C=\frac{n}{V}$

we get:

$\boxed{\pi=\frac{n}{V}RT}$

---

# Osmotic Pressure in Terms of Mass

Since:

$n=\frac{w_2}{M_2}$

we get:

$\pi=\frac{w_2}{M_2V}RT$

Therefore:

$\boxed{M_2=\frac{w_2RT}{\pi V}}$

This is one of the most important formulas in the chapter.

---

# Why Osmotic Pressure is Important

Osmotic pressure is particularly useful for finding molar masses of:

- Proteins
    
- Polymers
    
- Biological macromolecules
    

because it can be measured at room temperature and uses very dilute solutions.

---

# Reverse Osmosis

If pressure greater than the osmotic pressure is applied to the solution side:

$\boxed{P>\pi}$

solvent flows from the solution towards the pure solvent side.

This process is called:

**Reverse osmosis**

---

# Application of Reverse Osmosis

Reverse osmosis is used for:

**Desalination of seawater**

High pressure is applied to seawater, forcing water through a semipermeable membrane while many dissolved salts are retained.

---

# Isotonic Solutions

Two solutions having the same osmotic pressure at the same temperature are called:

**Isotonic solutions**

Therefore:

$\pi_1=\pi_2$

For dilute solutions:

$C_1RT=C_2RT$

Therefore:

$\boxed{C_1=C_2}$

assuming the same temperature and ideal behaviour.

---

# Hypertonic and Hypotonic

### Hypertonic solution

Has greater osmotic pressure.

$\pi_{hyper}>\pi_{hypo}$

Water tends to move towards the hypertonic solution.

### Hypotonic solution

Has lower osmotic pressure.

Water tends to move out of the hypotonic side towards the hypertonic side.

---

# Colligative Properties Summary

|Property|Formula|
|---|---|
|Relative lowering of vapour pressure|$\frac{\Delta p}{p^0}=x_2$|
|Elevation of boiling point|$\Delta T_b=K_bm$|
|Depression of freezing point|$\Delta T_f=K_fm$|
|Osmotic pressure|$\pi=CRT$|

---

# Abnormal Molar Mass

Sometimes experimentally determined molar mass differs from the expected molar mass.

This happens because solute particles may:

- Associate
    
- Dissociate
    

Therefore the actual number of particles differs from what we expect.

This causes **abnormal colligative properties**.

---

# Van't Hoff Factor

The van't Hoff factor is:

$\boxed{i=\frac{\text{Observed colligative property}}{\text{Calculated colligative property}}}$

It can also be written as:

$\boxed{i=\frac{\text{Normal molar mass}}{\text{Observed molar mass}}}$

---

# Modified Colligative Property Equations

For abnormal behaviour:

$\boxed{\frac{\Delta p}{p^0}=ix_2}$

$\boxed{\Delta T_b=iK_bm}$

$\boxed{\Delta T_f=iK_fm}$

$\boxed{\pi=iCRT}$

These are **extremely important for JEE**.

---

# When $i>1$

If the solute **dissociates**, the number of particles increases.

Therefore:

$\boxed{i>1}$

Example:

$NaCl\rightarrow Na^++Cl^-$

One particle becomes two.

---

# When $i<1$

If the solute **associates**, the number of particles decreases.

Therefore:

$\boxed{i<1}$

Example:

$2CH_3COOH\rightleftharpoons(CH_3COOH)_2$

Two particles combine to form one particle.

---

# When $i=1$

No association or dissociation occurs.

Therefore:

$\boxed{i=1}$

Example:

Glucose in dilute aqueous solution.

---

# Degree of Dissociation

Suppose one mole of electrolyte dissociates into $\nu$ particles.

If degree of dissociation is $\alpha$:

Initial particles:

$1$

After dissociation:

$1-\alpha+\nu\alpha$

Therefore:

$i=1-\alpha+\nu\alpha$

Hence:

$\boxed{i=1+\alpha(\nu-1)}$

Therefore:

$\boxed{\alpha=\frac{i-1}{\nu-1}}$

---

# Example: NaCl

$NaCl\rightarrow Na^++Cl^-$

Here:

$\nu=2$

Therefore:

$i=1+\alpha$

If complete dissociation occurs:

$\alpha=1$

so:

$i=2$

---

# Example: $CaCl_2$

$CaCl_2\rightarrow Ca^{2+}+2Cl^-$

Total particles:

$\nu=3$

Therefore:

$\boxed{i=1+2\alpha}$

For complete dissociation:

$\alpha=1$

so:

$i=3$

---

# Degree of Association

Suppose $n$ molecules associate to form one molecule:

$nA\rightleftharpoons A_n$

If degree of association is $\alpha$:

$i=1-\alpha+\frac{\alpha}{n}$

Therefore:

$\boxed{i=1-\alpha\left(1-\frac1n\right)}$

or:

$\boxed{i=1-\frac{(n-1)\alpha}{n}}$

---

# Dimerisation

A particularly important case is dimerisation:

$2A\rightleftharpoons A_2$

Here:

$n=2$

Therefore:

$\boxed{i=1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}$

Hence:

$\boxed{\alpha=2(1-i)}$

---

# Association and Molar Mass

Since:

$i=\frac{M_{normal}}{M_{observed}}$

if association occurs:

$i<1$

Therefore:

$M_{observed}>M_{normal}$

So the observed molar mass becomes **larger**.

---

# Dissociation and Molar Mass

If dissociation occurs:

$i>1$

Therefore:

$M_{observed}<M_{normal}$

So the observed molar mass becomes **smaller**.

---

# JEE Shortcut: Particle Counting

Whenever you see a colligative-property question involving an electrolyte, first ask:

**How many particles are produced?**

For example:

$NaCl\rightarrow2\text{ particles}$

$CaCl_2\rightarrow3\text{ particles}$

$AlCl_3\rightarrow4\text{ particles}$

$Na_2SO_4\rightarrow3\text{ particles}$

If complete dissociation occurs:

$i=\text{number of particles produced}$

---

# Important JEE Concept: Complete vs Partial Dissociation

Do not automatically use the number of ions as $i$.

For example:

$CaCl_2\rightleftharpoons Ca^{2+}+2Cl^-$

If only $50%$ dissociates:

$\alpha=0.5$

Then:

$i=1+2(0.5)$

$i=2$

not $3$.

---

# Molar Mass from Colligative Properties

For elevation of boiling point:

$\Delta T_b=iK_bm$

Substitute:

$m=\frac{1000w_2}{M_2w_1}$

Therefore:

$\Delta T_b=iK_b\frac{1000w_2}{M_2w_1}$

Thus:

$\boxed{M_2=\frac{iK_b1000w_2}{\Delta T_bw_1}}$

---

# Molar Mass from Depression in Freezing Point

$\Delta T_f=iK_fm$

Therefore:

$\boxed{M_2=\frac{iK_f1000w_2}{\Delta T_fw_1}}$

---

# Molar Mass from Osmotic Pressure

$\pi=i\frac{w_2}{M_2V}RT$

Therefore:

$\boxed{M_2=\frac{iw_2RT}{\pi V}}$

---

# Molar Mass from Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure

$\frac{\Delta p}{p^0}=ix_2$

For a dilute solution:

$\frac{\Delta p}{p^0}=i\frac{n_2}{n_1}$

Therefore:

$\frac{\Delta p}{p^0}=i\frac{w_2/M_2}{w_1/M_1}$

Hence:

$\boxed{M_2=\frac{iw_2M_1p^0}{w_1\Delta p}}$

---

# Important Graph Concepts

## Ideal Solution

Partial vapour pressure vs mole fraction is linear.

Total vapour pressure lies between the vapour pressures of the pure components.

---

## Positive Deviation

The total vapour pressure curve lies **above** the ideal Raoult's-law line.

Therefore:

$\boxed{P_{actual}>P_{ideal}}$

---

## Negative Deviation

The total vapour pressure curve lies **below** the ideal Raoult's-law line.

Therefore:

$\boxed{P_{actual}<P_{ideal}}$

---

# Boiling Point and Vapour Pressure Relationship

At a fixed external pressure:

Higher vapour pressure at a given temperature means the liquid reaches the boiling condition sooner.

Therefore:

### Positive deviation

$\text{Vapour pressure}\uparrow$

$\text{Boiling point}\downarrow$

### Negative deviation

$\text{Vapour pressure}\downarrow$

$\text{Boiling point}\uparrow$

---

# Important Concept: Volatile Solute

If both components are volatile:

$p_1=x_1p_1^0$

$p_2=x_2p_2^0$

and:

$p_{total}=p_1+p_2$

Do **not** use:

$\frac{\Delta p}{p^0}=x_{solute}$

directly unless the solute is **non-volatile**.

---

# Important Concept: Dilute Solution Approximation

For dilute solutions:

$n_{solute}\ll n_{solvent}$

Therefore:

$n_{solvent}+n_{solute}\approx n_{solvent}$

Hence:

$x_{solute}\approx\frac{n_{solute}}{n_{solvent}}$

This approximation is used extensively in derivations.

---

# Important JEE Concept: Mole Fraction After Dissociation

Suppose $1$ mole of $NaCl$ is dissolved and dissociates completely:

$NaCl\rightarrow Na^++Cl^-$

The number of solute particles becomes:

$2$

For colligative properties, count the **actual particles**, not the original formula units.

This is exactly why the van't Hoff factor is required.

---

# Important JEE Concept: Mixing Solutions

If two solutions containing the same solute are mixed, first calculate:

ntotal=n1+n2n_{total}=n_1+n_2

and:

Vtotal=V1+V2V_{total}=V_1+V_2

assuming volumes are additive.

Then:

$M_{final}=\frac{n_{total}}{V_{total}}$

For dilution:

$\boxed{M_1V_1=M_2V_2}$

This is frequently used in solution-preparation questions.

# Dilution

When solvent is added, the number of moles of solute remains constant.

Therefore:

$n_1=n_2$

Since:

$n=MV$

we get:

$\boxed{M_1V_1=M_2V_2}$

This relation is valid when the same solute is simply diluted.

---

# Important Difference: Molarity vs Mole Fraction

Molarity:

$M=\frac{\text{moles solute}}{\text{L solution}}$

Mole fraction:

$x_i=\frac{n_i}{\sum n}$

Molarity depends on **volume**.

Mole fraction depends on **moles**.

---

# Important Difference: Molarity vs Molality

$M=\frac{\text{moles solute}}{\text{L solution}}$

$m=\frac{\text{moles solute}}{\text{kg solvent}}$

Molarity is temperature dependent.

Molality is temperature independent.

### Relative lowering of vapour pressure

$\boxed{\Delta T_b=iK_bm}$

### Depression of freezing point

With van't Hoff factor:

$\boxed{\Delta T_f=iK_fm}$

### Osmotic pressure

$\pi=CRT$

$\pi=\frac{nRT}{V}$

With van't Hoff factor:

$\boxed{\pi=iCRT}$

### Van't Hoff factor

$i=\frac{\text{Observed colligative property}}{\text{Calculated colligative property}}$

$i=\frac{M_{normal}}{M_{observed}}$

### Dimerisation

# 🔥 JEE Must-Know Points

### Remember these relationships

**Higher $K_H$ → lower gas solubility**

$K_H\uparrow\Rightarrow x\downarrow$

---

**Positive deviation:**

$A-B$ weaker

$\Delta H_{mix}>0$

Vapour pressure $\uparrow$

Boiling point $\downarrow$

---

**Negative deviation:**

$A-B$ stronger

$\Delta H_{mix}<0$

Vapour pressure $\downarrow$

Boiling point $\uparrow$

---

**Dissociation:**

Number of particles $\uparrow$

$i>1$

$M_{observed}<M_{normal}$

---

**Association:**

Number of particles $\downarrow$

$i<1$

$M_{observed}>M_{normal}$

---

**No association/dissociation:**

$i=1$

---

**Molarity:**

Uses **volume of solution**

---

**Molality:**

Uses **mass of solvent**

---

$\pi=CRT$

and is particularly useful for finding molar masses of **macromolecules**.
