## 1. Reaction Mechanism Basics

In organic chemistry, a reaction typically follows this general path:

$$\text{Substrate (Reactant)} + \text{Reagent} \longrightarrow \text{Product}$$

---

## 2. Electrophiles ($E^+$)

Species that are "electron lovers." They are electron-deficient and attack nucleophilic (electron-rich) sites.

### Types and Examples:

- **Positively Charged:** $Cl^+$, $Br^+$, $CH_3^+$, $H^+$.
    
    - _Note:_ Not all positive species are electrophiles. $NH_4^+$ and $H_3O^+$ have complete octets and only act as electrophiles in aqueous conditions where they can release a proton ($H^+$).
        
- **Incomplete Octet:** $BF_3$, $BCl_3$, $AlCl_3$, $BeCl_2$.
    
    - **Order of Electrophilicity:** $BF_3 < BCl_3 < BBr_3 < BI_3$.
        
    - _Reason:_ In $BF_3$, there is $2p-2p$ back-bonding. In $BI_3$, the $2p-5p$ overlap is weak, making the Boron center more electron-deficient and thus a stronger electrophile.
        
- **Polar Functional Groups:** Species with a partial positive charge ($\delta^+$) on a carbon atom, such as Carbonyls ($C=O$) or Nitriles ($C \equiv N$).
    

### Lewis Acids:

Electron-deficient species that accept a pair of electrons. **All Lewis acids are electrophiles**, but not all electrophiles are Lewis acids (e.g., $CH_3^+$).

---

## 3. Nucleophiles ($Nu^-$)

Species that are "nucleus lovers." They are electron-rich and attack electrophilic centers.

### Types and Examples:

- **Negatively Charged:** $Cl^-$, $Br^-$, $NH_2^-$, $OH^-$, $CH_3^-$, $C_2H_5O^-$.
    
- **Neutral (with lone pairs):** $H_2O$, $NH_3$, $R\text{-}OH$, $R\text{-}NH_2$.
    
- **Organometallic Compounds:** $R\text{-}MgX$ (Grignard Reagent), $R_2Zn$ (Frankland Reagent), $R_2CuLi$ (Gilman's Reagent), $R\text{-}Li$.
    
- **$\pi$-electron rich species:** Alkenes ($C=C$), Alkynes ($C \equiv C$), and Benzene.
    

### Nucleophile vs. Lewis Base:

- **Lewis Base:** Attacks an $H^+$ (proton).
    
- **Nucleophile:** Attacks any electrophilic center other than $H^+$.
    

---

## 4. Periodic Trends

### Group 15 Hydrides ($NH_3$ to $SbH_3$):

- **Basic Character:** $NH_3 > PH_3 > AsH_3 > SbH_3$. (Small nitrogen holds lone pairs more densely; $Sb$ is bulky and donation is harder).
    
- **Nucleophilic Character:** $NH_3 < PH_3 < AsH_3 < SbH_3$.
    

### Period 2 Species ($CH_3^-$ to $F^-$):

- **Basic & Nucleophilic Character:** $CH_3^- > NH_2^- > OH^- > F^-$.
    
    - _Reason:_ Electronegativity (EN) increases from C to F. Higher EN means the atom holds onto its negative charge more tightly, making it less likely to donate electrons.
        

---

## 5. Nucleophilic Substitution ($S_N$)

General reaction:

$$Nu^- + R\text{-}X \longrightarrow R\text{-}Nu + X^-$$

Here, $X^-$ is the **Leaving Group (L.G.)**.

### Leaving Group Ability:

The rate of reaction is proportional to the stability of the leaving group. A weaker base is a better leaving group.

- **Halogens:** $I^- > Br^- > Cl^- > F^-$.
    
    - $HI$ is a very strong acid, meaning $I^-$ is a very weak, stable base and thus the **best** leaving group.
        
- **Stability:** $OH^- > NH_2^-$ because Oxygen is more electronegative than Nitrogen and can stabilize the negative charge better.

## **Stability of Gem-diols**

A **gem-diol** (geminal diol) features two hydroxyl ($-\text{OH}$) groups on the exact same carbon atom. Generally, these are unstable and eliminate water to reform a carbonyl group ($\text{C=O}$).

$$\text{C=O} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{C(OH)}_2$$

- **When $K_{\text{eq}} \uparrow$:** The forward reaction is favored, meaning the gem-diol is **stable**.
    
- **When $K_{\text{eq}} \downarrow$:** The backward reaction is favored, meaning the gem-diol is **unstable**.
    

### **Key Principles**

1. **Stability Trends:**
    
    $$\text{Stability} \propto \frac{1}{\text{EDG}} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Bulkiness}} \propto \text{EWG}$$
    
    _(Where $\text{EDG} =$ Electron Donating Group, and $\text{EWG} =$ Electron Withdrawing Group)_
    
2. **Reactivity towards $\text{H}_2\text{O}$:**
    
    $$\text{Aldehydes} > \text{Ketones}$$
    

### **Examples of Exceptionally Stable Gem-diols**

#### **1) Chloral Hydrate ($\text{CCl}_3\text{-CH(OH)}_2$)**

Formed from Chloral ($\text{CCl}_3\text{-CHO}$), it becomes highly stable due to **intramolecular hydrogen bonding** forming stable 5-membered rings.

Plaintext

```
       Cl       O - - - H
         \     /
          C - CH
         /     \
       Cl       O - - - H
```

#### **2) Ninhydrin Hydrate**

Ninhydrin forms a stable gem-diol at its central carbonyl group to relieve adjacent steric/carbonyl repulsions, locked in place by **intramolecular hydrogen bonding**.

Plaintext

```
         O - - - H
        /
   //--C 
  ||   | \ C(OH)2 
   \\--C /
        \
         O - - - H
```

#### **3) Formaldehyde Hydrate ($\text{HCHO}$)**

Formaldehyde readily hydrates because it completely lacks steric hindrance (no bulky groups) and lacks any $+I$ inductive stabilization on the carbonyl carbon.

$$\text{H}_2\text{C=O} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{C(OH)}_2$$

#### **4) Cyclopropanone Hydrate**

In cyclopropanone, the ideal bond angle for the $sp^2$ carbonyl carbon is $120^\circ$, but it is tightly forced into a $60^\circ$ ring geometry, creating severe ring strain. Hydration converts it to $sp^3$ where the ideal angle is $109.5^\circ$.

$$\text{Stability is driven by the } \textbf{reduction in ring strain.}$$

#### **5) Hexafluoroacetone Hydrate ($\text{CF}_3\text{-C(OH)}_2\text{-CF}_3$)**

The strong $-I$ (electron-withdrawing) effect of the two $-\text{CF}_3$ groups destabilizes the partial positive charge on the carbonyl carbon, shifting the equilibrium completely toward the stable gem-diol.

#### **6) Polycarbonyl Compounds (e.g., Triketones)**

$$\text{Ph-C(=O)-C(=O)-C(=O)-Ph}$$

The central carbonyl group undergoes hydration preferentially to relieve the severe electrostatic repulsion between adjacent dipole moments ($\text{C}^\delta\!=\!\text{O}^\delta$).

## **Haloform Reaction**

This reaction is used for the detection and conversion of **methyl ketones** ($\text{R-CO-CH}_3$).

### **Reagents Used**

1. $\text{X}_2 + \text{NaOH}$ (or $\text{KOH}$)
    
2. $\text{NaOX}$ (Sodium hypohalite)
    
3. $\text{CaOCl}_2$ (Bleaching powder)
    

### **Reaction Steps**

- **Step 1: Halogenation**
    
    $$\text{R-C(=O)-CH}_3 + 3\text{X}_2 \longrightarrow \text{R-C(=O)-CX}_3 + 3\text{HX}$$
    
- **Step 2: Alkaline Hydrolysis**
    
    $$\text{R-C(=O)-CX}_3 \xrightarrow{\text{OH}^-} \text{R-C(=O)-ONa} + \text{CHX}_3$$
    
    _(Where $\text{CHX}_3$ is the Haloform product)_
    

## **Reaction Mechanism**

### **1. $\alpha$-Halogenation (Repeated 3 times)**

The hydroxide base abstracts an acidic $\alpha$-hydrogen to form an enolate nucleophile, which subsequently attacks the molecular halogen ($\text{X}_2$).

$$\text{R-C(=O)-CH}_3 + \text{OH}^- \rightleftharpoons \text{R-C(=O)-CH}_2^- + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

$$\text{R-C(=O)-CH}_2^- + \text{X-X} \longrightarrow \text{R-C(=O)-CH}_2\text{X} + \text{X}^-$$

$$\xrightarrow{\text{Repeat 2 more times}} \text{R-C(=O)-CX}_3$$

### **2. Nucleophilic Acyl Substitution**

The hydroxide ion attacks the carbonyl carbon of the newly formed trihalomethyl ketone.

$$\text{R-C(=O)-CX}_3 + \text{OH}^- \longrightarrow \text{R-C(O^-)(OH)-CX}_3$$

### **3. Elimination of Trihalomethyl Carbanion**

The carbonyl $\pi$-bond reforms, expelling the stable $\text{^-\!CX}_3$ leaving group.

$$\text{R-C(O^-)(OH)-CX}_3\longrightarrow\text{R-C(=O)-OH} + \text{^-\!CX}_3$$

### **4. Proton Transfer**

An immediate acid-base reaction occurs between the newly formed carboxylic acid and the strongly basic carbanion, yielding the final carboxylate anion and the haloform precipitate.

$$\text{R-C(=O)-OH} + \text{^-\!CX}_3 \longrightarrow \text{R-C(=O)-O}^- + \text{CHX}_3$$
