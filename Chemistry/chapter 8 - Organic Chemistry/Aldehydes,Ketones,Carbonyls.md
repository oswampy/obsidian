## **Structure and Derivatives of Carboxylic Acids**

Carboxylic acids serve as the foundational structure for several important functional groups known as acid derivatives. In these derivatives, the hydroxyl ($-\text{OH}$) group of the carboxylic acid is replaced by another atom or group:

- **Acyl Halides ($\text{R}-\overset{\overset{\text{O}}{\parallel}}{\text{C}}-\text{X}$):** The hydroxyl group is replaced by a halogen atom ($\text{X} = \text{Cl}, \text{Br}, \text{I}$).
    
- **Acid Anhydrides ($\text{R}-\overset{\overset{\text{O}}{\parallel}}{\text{C}}-\text{O}-\overset{\overset{\text{O}}{\parallel}}{\text{C}}-\text{R}'$):** Formed when two carboxylic acid molecules condense and lose a water molecule, linking them via an oxygen bridge.
    
- **Amides ($\text{R}-\overset{\overset{\text{O}}{\parallel}}{\text{C}}-\text{NH}_2$):** The hydroxyl group is replaced by an amino group ($-\text{NH}_2$).
    
- **Esters ($\text{R}-\overset{\overset{\text{O}}{\parallel}}{\text{C}}-\text{OR}'$):** The hydrogen of the hydroxyl group is replaced by an alkyl or aryl carbon chain ($\text{R}'$).
    

## **Physical Properties of Carbonyls**

### **Boiling Points**

Aldehydes and ketones experience dipole-dipole interactions because of the polar carbonyl group ($\text{C}=\text{O}$), which makes their boiling points higher than similarly sized, non-polar hydrocarbons or weakly polar ethers.

However, because they lack a hydrogen atom directly bonded to an oxygen, they cannot form intermolecular hydrogen bonds with themselves. Consequently, their boiling points are lower than those of corresponding alcohols:

$$\text{alcohol} > \text{ketone} > \text{aldehyde} > \text{ether} > \text{hydrocarbon}$$

### **Solubility**

- **Lower members** (like formaldehyde or acetone) are completely miscible in water because they can readily form hydrogen bonds with water molecules.
    
- **Higher members** become increasingly immiscible in water. As the non-polar alkyl chain grows longer, the hydrophobic portion prevents effective hydrogen bonding with water.
    
- All carbonyl compounds are soluble in common organic solvents.
    

## **General Methods of Preparation (MOP)**

1. **Oxidation of Alcohols:** Primary alcohols are oxidized to yield aldehydes ($\text{R}-\text{OH} \rightarrow \text{RCHO}$), while secondary alcohols oxidize to yield ketones.
    
2. **Reductive Ozonolysis:** Passing ozone ($\text{O}_3$) through an alkene in the presence of a reducing agent like zinc ($\text{Zn}$) or dimethyl sulfide ($\text{Me}_2\text{S}$) cleaves the carbon-carbon double bond completely, transforming it into two carbonyl groups via a cyclic **ozonide** intermediate:
    
    $$\text{C}=\text{C} \xrightarrow{\text{O}_3 / \text{Zn} + \text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{C}=\text{O} + \text{O}=\text{C}$$
    
3. **Kucherov Reaction:** The hydration of alkynes using a mercury catalyst ($\text{Hg}^{2+}/\text{H}^+$). Alkynes add water across the triple bond to form an unstable **enol** intermediate, which rapidly tautomerizes into its more stable ketone or aldehyde form:
    
    $$\text{R}-\text{C}\equiv\text{C}-\text{H} \xrightarrow{\text{Hg}^{2+}/\text{H}_2\text{O}} \left[ \text{R}-\overset{\overset{\text{OH}}{\mid}}{\text{C}}=\text{CH}_2 \right] \rightleftharpoons \text{R}-\overset{\overset{\text{O}}{\parallel}}{\text{C}}-\text{CH}_3$$
    
4. **Dry Distillation of Calcium Salts:** Heating calcium salts of carboxylic acids causes thermal decomposition, yielding ketones or aldehydes alongside a calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$) byproduct:
    
    $$\text{(RCOO)}_2\text{Ca} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{R}-\overset{\overset{\text{O}}{\parallel}}{\text{C}}-\text{R} + \text{CaCO}_3$$
    
5. **From Carboxylic Acids Directly:** Passing carboxylic acid vapors over a manganese dioxide ($\text{MnO}$) catalyst at high temperatures ($300^\circ\text{C}$) results in decarboxylation and dehydration to form a carbonyl compound:
    
    $$\text{R}-\text{COOH} + \text{R}-\text{COOH} \xrightarrow[300^\circ\text{C}]{\text{MnO}} \text{R}-\overset{\overset{\text{O}}{\parallel}}{\text{C}}-\text{R} + \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
    

### **Periodic Acid Oxidation (Malaprade Reaction)**

Periodic acid ($\text{HIO}_4$) is a highly selective oxidizing agent used to cleave carbon-carbon bonds in specific adjacent (vicinal) configurations.

#### **Functional Groups Cleaved:**

- **Vicinal diols (1,2-diols):** $\begin{matrix} \text{C}-\text{OH} \\ \mid \\ \text{C}-\text{OH} \end{matrix}$
    
- **$\alpha$-hydroxy carbonyls:** $\begin{matrix} \text{C}-\text{OH} \\ \mid \\ \text{C}=\text{O} \end{matrix}$
    
- **1,2-diketones:** $\begin{matrix} \text{C}=\text{O} \\ \mid \\ \text{C}=\text{O} \end{matrix}$
    
- **$\alpha$-amino alcohols:** $\begin{matrix} \text{C}-\text{OH} \\ \mid \\ \text{C}-\text{NH}_2 \end{matrix}$
    

#### **Groups Immune to Cleavage:**

- **Ether derivatives:** $\begin{matrix} \text{C}-\text{OH} \\ \mid \\ \text{C}-\text{OR} \end{matrix}$ or $\begin{matrix} \text{C}-\text{OR} \\ \mid \\ \text{C}-\text{OR} \end{matrix}$
    
- **Isolated functional groups** separated by one or more plain carbon atoms.
    
- **_trans_-1,2-diols** locked on rigid ring systems where the two hydroxyl groups are physically too far apart to interact with the reagent.
    

#### **Mechanism and The "OH Addition" Rule**

The reaction proceeds via a coordinate step where a **5-membered cyclic iodine ester intermediate** is formed.

To easily predict the final oxidized products of a complex polyol (like glucose), apply the cleavage rule: **break every vicinal $\text{C}-\text{C}$ bond and add a new $-\text{OH}$ group to each of the separated carbons**.

If a single carbon atom ends up with multiple $-\text{OH}$ groups, it is unstable and immediately loses a molecule of water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) to form a stable carbonyl ($\text{C}=\text{O}$) or carboxylic acid ($\text{COOH}$) group. For example, a multi-hydroxyl chain will yield several moles of formic acid ($\text{HCOOH}$) and formaldehyde ($\text{HCHO}$):

$$\text{Polyol Chain} \xrightarrow{\text{HIO}_4} 5\text{HCOOH} + \text{HCHO}$$

## **Specific Synthesis of Aldehydes**

1. **Rosenmund Reduction:** An acyl chloride is selectively reduced to an aldehyde using hydrogen gas over a palladium catalyst supported by barium sulfate ($\text{Pd/BaSO}_4$). The barium sulfate acts to "poison" or deactivate the palladium, preventing the highly reactive aldehyde product from being further reduced into a primary alcohol:
    
    $$\text{R}-\overset{\overset{\text{O}}{\parallel}}{\text{C}}-\text{Cl} \xrightarrow{\text{H}_2 + \text{Pd/BaSO}_4} \text{R}-\overset{\overset{\text{O}}{\parallel}}{\text{C}}-\text{H}$$
    
2. **Stephen Reduction:** Nitriles ($\text{R}-\text{CN}$) are reduced using tin(II) chloride and hydrochloric acid ($\text{SnCl}_2/\text{HCl}$) to form an imine hydrochloride salt intermediate ($\text{R}-\text{CH}=\text{NH}$). Subsequent acid hydrolysis with steam decomposes this intermediate to yield the final aldehyde product:
    
    $$\text{R}-\text{C}\equiv\text{N} \xrightarrow{\text{SnCl}_2 + \text{HCl}} \text{R}-\text{CH}=\text{NH} \xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{R}-\text{CHO} + \text{NH}_3$$
    
3. **Reduction by DIBAL-H:** Diisobutylaluminum hydride ($\text{DIBAL-H}$) is a sterically hindered, electrophilic reducing agent. At very low temperatures, it can selectively reduce nitriles, esters, or acyl chlorides to an imine or intermediate stage, which stops completely before over-reduction happens. Simple hydrolysis then frees the aldehyde:
    
    $$\text{R}-\text{C}\equiv\text{N} \xrightarrow{\text{DIBAL-H}} \text{R}-\text{CH}=\text{NH} \xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{R}-\text{CHO}$$
    
4. **Etard Reaction:** Direct conversion of a methyl group on an aromatic ring into an aldehyde using chromyl chloride ($\text{CrO}_2\text{Cl}_2$). The reaction forms a brown precipitate consisting of a chromium complex intermediate, which is isolated and hydrolyzed to yield benzaldehyde:
    
    $$\text{Ph}-\text{CH}_3 \xrightarrow{\text{CrO}_2\text{Cl}_2} \text{Ph}-\text{CH(OCrCl}_2\text{OH)}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{Ph}-\text{CHO}$$
    
5. **Side-Chain Chlorination:** Toluene undergoes free-radical chlorination controlled to substitute exactly two hydrogens, forming benzal chloride. Treatment with aqueous base converts the geminal dihalide into an unstable gem-diol intermediate, which instantly dehydrates into a stable benzaldehyde:
    
    $$\text{Ph}-\text{CH}_3 \xrightarrow{2\text{Cl}_2 / h\nu} \text{Ph}-\text{CHCl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{aq. KOH}} \left[ \text{Ph}-\text{CH(OH)}_2 \right] \xrightarrow{-\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{Ph}-\text{CHO}$$
    
6. **Gattermann-Koch Reaction:** Formyl chloride ($\text{H-CO-Cl}$) is highly unstable and cannot be stored. In this reaction, it is formulated _in situ_ by passing a high-pressure stream of carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$) and hydrogen chloride ($\text{HCl}$) gas into a solution of benzene containing an anhydrous aluminum chloride ($\text{AlCl}_3$) Lewis acid catalyst, electrophilically substituting a formyl group directly onto the ring:
    
    $$\text{Benzene} \xrightarrow{\text{CO} + \text{HCl} / \text{AlCl}_3} \text{Benzaldehyde}$$
