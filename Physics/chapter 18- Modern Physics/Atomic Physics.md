

### 5.1 Discovery of Atomic Structure

Atoms contain a small, dense nucleus surrounded by electrons.

The nucleus contains positively charged protons and neutral neutrons.

Most of the mass of an atom is concentrated in its nucleus.

### 5.2 Thomson's Atomic Model

According to Thomson:

- An atom was considered a sphere of positive charge.
    
- Electrons were embedded inside this positive sphere.
    
- The atom was electrically neutral overall.
    

This model could not explain the results of Rutherford's scattering experiment.

### 5.3 Rutherford's Alpha-Particle Scattering Experiment

Rutherford directed alpha particles towards a very thin gold foil.

#### Observations

1. Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil.
    
2. Some particles were deflected through small angles.
    
3. Very few particles were deflected through large angles.
    
4. A very small number returned almost in the backward direction.
    

#### Conclusions

- Most of the atom is empty space.
    
- Positive charge is concentrated in a very small central region.
    
- Almost the entire mass of the atom is concentrated in the nucleus.
    
- The nucleus is positively charged.
    

### 5.4 Rutherford's Atomic Model

According to Rutherford:

- Electrons revolve around a positively charged nucleus.
    
- Electrostatic attraction provides the centripetal force.
    
- Most of the atomic volume is empty space.
    

#### Limitations

According to classical electromagnetic theory, an accelerating charged particle should continuously radiate energy.

Therefore, an electron revolving around the nucleus should lose energy and spiral into the nucleus.

This would make atoms unstable, which is not observed.

Rutherford's model also could not explain the discrete line spectra of atoms.

## 6. Bohr's Atomic Model

### 6.1 Bohr's Postulates

Bohr proposed the following postulates:

1. Electrons revolve around the nucleus only in certain permitted circular orbits called stationary orbits.
    
2. Electrons do not radiate energy while moving in these stationary orbits.
    
3. The angular momentum of an electron is quantised.
    
4. Radiation is emitted or absorbed when an electron transitions between two permitted energy levels.
    

### 6.2 Quantisation of Angular Momentum

The angular momentum of an electron is:

$mvr=\dfrac{nh}{2\pi}$

Where:

- $m$ = mass of electron.
    
- $v$ = orbital velocity.
    
- $r$ = orbital radius.
    
- $n$ = principal quantum number.
    

### 6.3 Radius of nth Orbit

For a hydrogen-like atom, the electrostatic force provides the centripetal force.

$\dfrac{mv^2}{r}=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{Ze^2}{r^2}$

Using angular momentum quantisation:

$mvr=\dfrac{nh}{2\pi}$

Combining these equations:

$r_n=\dfrac{4\pi\epsilon_0 n^2h^2}{4\pi^2mZe^2}$

Therefore:

$r_n=\dfrac{a_0n^2}{Z}$

Where:

$a_0=0.529\ \mathring A$

For hydrogen:

$r_n=0.529n^2\ \mathring A$

**Important observations:**

- Radius is proportional to $n^2$.
    
- Radius is inversely proportional to nuclear charge $Z$.
    
- Higher orbits have larger radii.
    

### 6.4 Velocity of Electron in nth Orbit

The orbital velocity is:

$v_n=\dfrac{Ze^2}{2\epsilon_0nh}$

For hydrogen:

$v_n=\dfrac{2.18\times10^6}{n}\ m/s$

Therefore:

$v_n\propto\dfrac{Z}{n}$

### 6.5 Energy of Electron in nth Orbit

Total energy is the sum of kinetic and potential energies.

$E=K+U$

Kinetic energy:

$K=\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$

Potential energy:

$U=-\dfrac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r}$

Using the Bohr model:

$K=\dfrac{Ze^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0r}$

Therefore:

$E=-\dfrac{Ze^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0r}$

Substituting the radius:

$E_n=-\dfrac{13.6Z^2}{n^2}\ eV$

For hydrogen:

$E_n=-\dfrac{13.6}{n^2}\ eV$

**Important observations:**

- Energy is negative for bound electrons.
    
- Energy becomes less negative as $n$ increases.
    
- Energy is proportional to $-Z^2$.
    
- As $n\rightarrow\infty$, energy approaches zero.
    

### 6.6 Ionisation Energy

Ionisation energy is the minimum energy required to remove an electron completely from an atom in a particular energy state.

For hydrogen in the ground state:

$E_{ion}=13.6\ eV$

For a hydrogen-like species in the nth orbit:

$E_{ion}=\dfrac{13.6Z^2}{n^2}\ eV$

### 6.7 Excitation Energy

Excitation energy is the energy required to move an electron from a lower energy level to a higher energy level.

$E_{excitation}=E_{final}-E_{initial}$

For hydrogen, from $n=1$ to $n=2$:

$E_{excitation}=-3.4-(-13.6)$

$E_{excitation}=10.2\ eV$

### 6.8 Energy Difference Between Two Levels

For a transition from $n_i$ to $n_f$:

$\Delta E=E_f-E_i$

The energy of the photon emitted or absorbed is:

$h\nu=|E_f-E_i|$

Therefore:

$\dfrac{hc}{\lambda}=|E_f-E_i|$

### 6.9 Emission and Absorption

**Emission:**

- Electron moves from a higher energy level to a lower energy level.
    
- Energy is released as a photon.
    

**Absorption:**

- Electron moves from a lower energy level to a higher energy level.
    
- Energy is absorbed from an incident photon.
    

### 6.10 Hydrogen Spectrum

When electrons transition between energy levels, they produce spectral lines.

The wavelength is given by the Rydberg formula:

$\dfrac{1}{\lambda}=RZ^2\left(\dfrac{1}{n_f^2}-\dfrac{1}{n_i^2}\right)$

For emission:

$n_i>n_f$

For hydrogen, $Z=1$.

### 6.11 Spectral Series

|Series|Final orbit|Region|
|---|---|---|
|Lyman|$n_f=1$|Ultraviolet|
|Balmer|$n_f=2$|Visible and near ultraviolet|
|Paschen|$n_f=3$|Infrared|
|Brackett|$n_f=4$|Infrared|
|Pfund|$n_f=5$|Infrared|

### 6.12 Important Spectral Calculations

**Longest wavelength in a series**

This corresponds to the smallest energy difference.

The transition is from:

$n_i=n_f+1$

**Shortest wavelength in a series**

This corresponds to the largest possible energy difference.

The transition is from:

$n_i=\infty$

**Number of spectral lines**

If an electron is initially at the nth excited level and all possible downward transitions are considered:

$N=\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}$

Example:

If an electron is initially at $n=4$:

$N=\dfrac{4(3)}{2}=6$

### 6.13 Hydrogen-like Species

Species containing only one electron are called hydrogen-like species.

Examples:

- $H$
    
- $He^+$
    
- $Li^{2+}$
    
- $Be^{3+}$
    

For these species:

$r_n\propto\dfrac{n^2}{Z}$

$E_n\propto-\dfrac{Z^2}{n^2}$

$v_n\propto\dfrac{Z}{n}$

### 6.14 Comparison Between Hydrogen and Hydrogen-like Ions

For the same principal quantum number:

- Higher nuclear charge produces a smaller orbital radius.
    
- Higher nuclear charge produces a greater binding energy.
    
- Higher nuclear charge produces greater electron velocity.
    

### 6.15 Limitations of Bohr's Model

- It works well mainly for hydrogen-like atoms.
    
- It cannot explain the spectra of multi-electron atoms.
    
- It cannot fully explain the fine structure of spectral lines.
    
- It cannot explain the Zeeman and Stark effects completely.
    
- It assumes definite circular orbits, which is inconsistent with the modern quantum mechanical description.
