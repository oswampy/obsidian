## 2. Dual Nature of Radiation

### 2.1 Wave Nature of Light

Light behaves as an electromagnetic wave.

Wave properties of light include:

- Interference.
    
- Diffraction.
    
- Polarisation.
    

However, certain phenomena cannot be explained using only the wave theory of light.

The photoelectric effect demonstrates the particle nature of light.

### 2.2 Particle Nature of Light

According to the quantum theory, light consists of discrete packets of energy called **photons**.

Each photon has energy:

$E=h\nu=\dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$

Where:

- $h$ = Planck's constant.
    
- $\nu$ = frequency.
    
- $\lambda$ = wavelength.
    
- $c$ = speed of light.
    

### 2.3 Properties of Photons

1. Photons have zero rest mass.
    
2. They travel at the speed of light in vacuum.
    
3. They have energy $E=h\nu$.
    
4. They carry momentum despite having zero rest mass.
    
5. Their momentum is $p=\dfrac{h}{\lambda}$.
    
6. Their energy and momentum are related by $E=pc$.
    
7. They are electrically neutral.
    

### 2.4 Intensity of Light

**Intensity** is the energy transmitted per unit area per unit time.

For monochromatic light:

$I=\dfrac{N h\nu}{At}$

Where $N$ is the number of photons incident on area $A$ in time $t$.

Therefore:

- Increasing intensity increases the number of photons arriving per second.
    
- Increasing frequency increases the energy of each photon.
    
- Increasing intensity does not increase the energy of an individual photon.

## 3. Photoelectric Effect

### 3.1 Definition

The **photoelectric effect** is the phenomenon in which electrons are emitted from a metal surface when electromagnetic radiation of sufficiently high frequency falls on it.

The emitted electrons are called photoelectrons.

### 3.2 Important Terms

**Photoelectric current**

The current produced due to the flow of emitted photoelectrons.

**Work Function ($\phi$)**

The minimum energy required to remove an electron from the surface of a metal.

$\phi=h\nu_0$

Where $\nu_0$ is the threshold frequency.

**Threshold Frequency ($\nu_0$)**

The minimum frequency of incident radiation required to produce photoelectric emission.

**Threshold Wavelength ($\lambda_0$)**

The maximum wavelength of incident radiation that can produce photoelectric emission.

$\lambda_0=\dfrac{c}{\nu_0}$

**Stopping Potential ($V_0$)**

The minimum reverse potential required to stop even the most energetic photoelectrons from reaching the collector.

**Saturation Current**

The maximum photoelectric current obtained when all emitted photoelectrons are collected.

### 3.3 Laws of Photoelectric Emission

1. For a given metal, photoelectric emission occurs only when the frequency of incident radiation is greater than or equal to the threshold frequency.
    
2. The photoelectric current is directly proportional to the intensity of incident radiation, provided the frequency is above the threshold frequency.
    
3. The maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons depends on the frequency of incident radiation and is independent of its intensity.
    
4. Photoelectric emission is practically instantaneous, with no measurable time lag under ordinary experimental conditions.
    

### 3.4 Einstein's Photoelectric Equation

Einstein explained the photoelectric effect using the particle nature of light.

A photon transfers its entire energy to a single electron.

Part of this energy is used to overcome the work function, and the remaining energy becomes the kinetic energy of the emitted electron.

By conservation of energy:

$h\nu=\phi+K_{max}$

Therefore:

$K_{max}=h\nu-\phi$

Since $\phi=h\nu_0$:

$K_{max}=h(\nu-\nu_0)$

Using stopping potential:

$K_{max}=eV_0$

Hence:

$eV_0=h\nu-\phi$

### 3.5 Maximum Velocity of Photoelectrons

Using kinetic energy:

$\dfrac{1}{2}m_ev_{max}^2=h\nu-\phi$

Therefore:

$v_{max}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2(h\nu-\phi)}{m_e}}$

### 3.6 Photoelectric Effect Graphs

#### Graph 1: Kinetic Energy vs Frequency

The equation is:

$K_{max}=h\nu-\phi$

This is a straight line.

- Slope = $h$.
    
- X-intercept = $\nu_0$.
    
- Y-intercept = $-\phi$.
    

The slope is independent of the metal used.

#### Graph 2: Stopping Potential vs Frequency

$V_0=\dfrac{h}{e}\nu-\dfrac{\phi}{e}$

- Slope = $\dfrac{h}{e}$.
    
- X-intercept = threshold frequency.
    
- Different metals have different X-intercepts.
    

#### Graph 3: Photoelectric Current vs Applied Potential

- At positive collector potential, current increases and eventually reaches saturation.
    
- At negative collector potential, current decreases.
    
- At stopping potential, current becomes zero.
    

Increasing light intensity increases saturation current but does not change stopping potential.

#### Graph 4: Effect of Intensity

For the same frequency above threshold:

- Higher intensity produces more photoelectrons.
    
- Saturation current increases.
    
- Maximum kinetic energy remains unchanged.
    

#### Graph 5: Effect of Frequency

For the same intensity:

- Increasing frequency increases maximum kinetic energy.
    
- Stopping potential increases.
    
- Saturation current is not determined by frequency alone; it also depends on photon flux and collection conditions.
    

### 3.7 Why Classical Wave Theory Failed

Classical wave theory predicted that:

- Electron energy should increase with intensity.
    
- There should be a time delay at low intensities.
    
- There should be no threshold frequency.
    

Experimental observations contradicted these predictions.

Einstein's photon theory successfully explained all the observations.

### 3.8 Important Photoelectric Effect Results

**If intensity is doubled:**

- Number of incident photons per second doubles.
    
- Photoelectric current approximately doubles under the same collection conditions.
    
- Maximum kinetic energy remains constant.
    

**If frequency is increased:**

- Photon energy increases.
    
- Maximum kinetic energy increases.
    
- Stopping potential increases.
    

**If frequency is below threshold:**

No photoelectric emission occurs, regardless of how large the intensity is.

### 3.9 JEE-Level Photon Calculations

**Number of photons emitted per second**

If a source has power $P$ and emits monochromatic radiation of frequency $\nu$:

$N=\dfrac{P}{h\nu}$

If the wavelength is given:

$N=\dfrac{P\lambda}{hc}$

**Photoelectric current**

If $n$ electrons are emitted per second:

$I=ne$

If each incident photon has probability or quantum efficiency $\eta$ of producing an emitted electron:

$I=\eta\dfrac{Pe}{h\nu}$

This assumes all emitted electrons are collected.

**Stopping potential difference**

For two incident frequencies:

$e(V_2-V_1)=h(\nu_2-\nu_1)$

For the same metal, the work function cancels.

|Stopping potential vs frequency|Straight line with slope $h/e$|

### 16.2 de Broglie Wavelength

For a charged particle accelerated through potential $V$:

$\lambda\propto V^{-1/2}$

### 16.3 Bohr Model

|Quantity|Dependence|
|---|---|
|Radius|$r_n\propto n^2/Z$|
|Velocity|$v_n\propto Z/n$|
|Energy|$E_n\propto-Z^2/n^2$|
|Angular momentum|$L\propto n$|

### 16.4 Radioactive Decay

$N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$

The graph of number of undecayed nuclei against time is an exponential decay curve.

### 16.5 Binding Energy Curve

- Increases rapidly for light nuclei.
    
- Peaks near iron and nickel.
    
- Slowly decreases for heavier nuclei.
    

### 16.6 Diode Characteristics

- Forward bias: rapidly increasing current after the knee region.
    
- Reverse bias: small saturation current followed by breakdown.

## 17. Important Formula Sheet

### Dual Nature

Photon energy:

$E=h\nu=\dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$

Photon momentum:

$p=\dfrac{h}{\lambda}=\dfrac{E}{c}$

Photoelectric equation:

$K_{max}=h\nu-\phi$

Work function:

$\phi=h\nu_0$

Stopping potential:

$eV_0=K_{max}$

Maximum velocity:

$v_{max}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2K_{max}}{m_e}}$

Photon rate:

$N=\dfrac{P}{h\nu}$

### de Broglie Waves

$\lambda=\dfrac{h}{p}$

$\lambda=\dfrac{h}{mv}$

$\lambda=\dfrac{h}{\sqrt{2mK}}$

For an electron accelerated through V:

$\lambda=\dfrac{12.27}{\sqrt V}\ \mathring A$

Uncertainty principle:

$\Delta x\Delta p\geq\dfrac{\hbar}{2}$

### Bohr Model

Angular momentum:

$mvr=\dfrac{nh}{2\pi}$

Radius:

$r_n=\dfrac{a_0n^2}{Z}$

Velocity:

$v_n=\dfrac{2.18\times10^6Z}{n}\ m/s$

Energy:

$E_n=-\dfrac{13.6Z^2}{n^2}\ eV$

Ionisation energy:

$E_{ion}=\dfrac{13.6Z^2}{n^2}\ eV$

Rydberg equation:

$\dfrac{1}{\lambda}=RZ^2\left(\dfrac{1}{n_f^2}-\dfrac{1}{n_i^2}\right)$

Number of spectral lines:

$N=\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}$

### X-rays

$\lambda_{min}=\dfrac{hc}{eV}$

$\lambda_{min}(nm)=\dfrac{1240}{V}$

### Nuclear Physics

Mass number:

$A=Z+N$

Nuclear radius:

$R=R_0A^{1/3}$

Mass defect:

$\Delta m=Zm_p+(A-Z)m_n-M_{nucleus}$

Binding energy:

$BE=\Delta mc^2$

Binding energy in MeV:

$BE=\Delta m(u)\times931.5$

Binding energy per nucleon:

$BE/A$

Nuclear energy:

$Q=\Delta mc^2$

### Radioactivity

Decay law:

$N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$

Activity:

$A=\lambda N$

Half-life:

$T_{1/2}=\dfrac{0.693}{\lambda}$

Mean life:

$\tau=\dfrac{1}{\lambda}$

Relationship:

$T_{1/2}=0.693\tau$

### Semiconductor Electronics

Diode equation:

$I=I_s\left(e^{qV/\eta k_BT}-1\right)$

Half-wave average output:

$V_{dc}=\dfrac{V_m}{\pi}$

Full-wave average output:

$V_{dc}=\dfrac{2V_m}{\pi}$

$V_{rms}=\dfrac{V_m}{2}$

$V_{rms}=\dfrac{V_m}{\sqrt2}$

## 18. Common JEE Exam Traps

### 18.1 Photoelectric Effect

- Increasing intensity does not increase maximum kinetic energy.
    
- Increasing frequency increases maximum kinetic energy.
    
- Below threshold frequency, increasing intensity cannot produce photoelectric emission.
    
- Work function depends on the metal, not the incident light.
    
- Stopping potential depends on **frequency and work function.**
    

### 18.2 de Broglie Wavelength

- Same momentum means same wavelength, regardless of mass.
    
- Same kinetic energy does not mean same wavelength.
    
- For the same particle, doubling accelerating voltage does not halve wavelength.
    
- Relativistic corrections are needed when particle speed is comparable to the speed of light.
    

### 18.3 Bohr Model

- Higher n means a larger orbit.
    
- Higher n means energy is less negative.
    
- Ionisation energy decreases with increasing n.
    
- For the same n, higher Z means smaller radius and greater binding energy.
    
- Emission occurs when an electron moves down an energy level.
    

### 18.4 Nuclear Physics

- Atomic mass and nuclear mass are not the same.
    
- When using atomic masses, use hydrogen atomic mass consistently in the mass-defect equation.
    
- Higher binding energy per nucleon generally indicates greater nuclear stability.
    
- Nuclear radius varies as $A^{1/3}$, not A.
    
- The density of nuclei is approximately constant.
    

### 18.5 Radioactivity

- Half-life is independent of initial quantity.
    
- Activity is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei.
    
- Beta-minus decay increases atomic number by one.
    
- Beta-plus decay decreases atomic number by one.
    
- Gamma decay changes neither mass number nor atomic number.
    

### 18.6 Semiconductors

- n-type does not mean the semiconductor has an overall negative charge.
    
- p-type does not mean the semiconductor has an overall positive charge.
    
- Majority carriers determine the dominant type of conduction.
    
- Forward bias reduces the depletion width.
    
- Reverse bias increases the depletion width.
    
- An LED is normally forward biased.
    
- A photodiode is normally reverse biased.
    
- A solar cell works using the photovoltaic effect.
