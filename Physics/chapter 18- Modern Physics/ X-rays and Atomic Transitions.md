## 7.1 What Are X-rays?

X-rays are high-frequency electromagnetic waves with very short wavelengths.

They are produced when high-speed electrons interact with a metal target.

### 7.2 Production of X-rays

When electrons accelerated through a potential difference strike a metal target:

- Their kinetic energy is converted into electromagnetic radiation.
    
- Some energy produces continuous X-ray radiation.
    
- Some interactions produce characteristic X-ray lines.
    

### 7.3 Minimum Wavelength of X-rays

If an electron accelerated through potential difference $V$ converts all its kinetic energy into a single photon:

$eV=h\nu_{max}$

Since:

$\nu=\dfrac{c}{\lambda}$

Therefore:

$\lambda_{min}=\dfrac{hc}{eV}$

In convenient units:

$\lambda_{min}(nm)=\dfrac{1240}{V}$

Here, $V$ is measured in volts.

### 7.4 Continuous and Characteristic X-rays

**Continuous X-rays**

Produced when fast electrons are decelerated in the target material.

**Characteristic X-rays**

Produced when an inner-shell electron is removed and an electron from a higher energy shell fills the vacancy.

The emitted photon has energy equal to the difference between the two atomic energy levels.

### 7.5 Moseley's Law

Moseley's law relates the frequency of characteristic X-rays to the atomic number of the element.

$\sqrt{\nu}=a(Z-b)$

Where:

- $Z$ = atomic number.
    
- $a$ = constant.
    
- $b$ = screening constant.
    

For a particular characteristic series, frequency increases with atomic number.

**Important:** This is an additional atomic-physics topic, not a required derivation in the current CBSE Class 12 syllabus.

## 8. Nuclear Physics

### 8.1 Structure of the Nucleus

The nucleus contains protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons.

**Atomic number ($Z$)**

Number of protons in the nucleus.

**Mass number ($A$)**

Total number of protons and neutrons.

$A=Z+N$

Where $N$ is the number of neutrons.

Therefore:

$N=A-Z$

### 8.2 Nuclear Notation

A nucleus is represented as:

$^{A}_{Z}X$

Where:

- $X$ = chemical symbol.
    
- $A$ = mass number.
    
- $Z$ = atomic number.
    

Example:

$^{23}_{11}Na$

It contains:

- 11 protons.
    
- 12 neutrons.
    

### 8.3 Isotopes, Isobars and Isotones

**Isotopes**

Atoms having the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

Example:

$^1H,\ ^2H,\ ^3H$

**Isobars**

Atoms having the same mass number but different atomic numbers.

Example:

$^{40}Ar$ and $^{40}Ca$

**Isotones**

Atoms having the same number of neutrons but different atomic numbers.

Example:

$^{14}C$ and $^{15}N$

Both contain 8 neutrons.

### 8.4 Size of the Nucleus

The nuclear radius is given by:

$R=R_0A^{1/3}$

Where:

$R_0\approx1.2\times10^{-15}\ m$

Important conclusions:

- Nuclear radius is proportional to $A^{1/3}$.
    
- Nuclear volume is proportional to $A$.
    
- Nuclear density is approximately constant for different nuclei.
    

### 8.5 Nuclear Density

Density:

$\rho=\dfrac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}$

Approximating nuclear mass as $Am_n$:

$\rho=\dfrac{Am_n}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3}$

Since:

$R=R_0A^{1/3}$

Therefore:

$\rho=\dfrac{3m_n}{4\pi R_0^3}$

The mass number cancels.

**Hence, nuclear density is  independent of the mass number.**

### 8.6 Nuclear Force

Nuclear force is the strong attractive force that holds protons and neutrons together inside the nucleus.

Properties:

1. It is extremely strong.
    
2. It is short-ranged.
    
3. It is approximately charge-independent.
    
4. It is attractive at ordinary nuclear separations.
    
5. It becomes strongly repulsive at extremely small separations.
    
6. It shows saturation, meaning a nucleon interacts strongly with only a limited number of nearby nucleons.
    

### 8.7 Mass-Energy Equivalence

According to Einstein:

$E=mc^2$

Mass and energy are interchangeable.

A small amount of mass corresponds to a large amount of energy because the speed of light is very large.

For nuclear reactions, the energy released is:

$Q=\Delta mc^2$

## 9. Mass Defect and Binding Energy

### 9.1 Mass Defect

The mass of a nucleus is less than the total mass of its individual free nucleons.

The difference is called **mass defect**.

$\Delta m=Zm_p+(A-Z)m_n-M_{nucleus}$

Where:

- $Z$ = number of protons.
    
- $A-Z$ = number of neutrons.
    
- $m_p$ = proton mass.
    
- $m_n$ = neutron mass.
    
- $M_{nucleus}$ = actual nuclear mass.
    

### 9.2 Binding Energy

Binding energy is the energy required to separate a nucleus completely into its individual protons and neutrons.

$BE=\Delta mc^2$

If mass defect is expressed in atomic mass units:

$BE=\Delta m\times931.5\ MeV$

### 9.3 Binding Energy per Nucleon

It is the average binding energy associated with each nucleon.

$BE/A=\dfrac{\text{Total binding energy}}{\text{Mass number}}$

It is an indicator of nuclear stability.

Generally, higher binding energy per nucleon indicates a more stable nucleus.

### 9.4 Binding Energy Curve

The binding energy per nucleon generally:

- Increases rapidly for light nuclei.
    
- Reaches a maximum near iron and nickel.
    
- Decreases slowly for heavy nuclei.
    

The maximum is approximately $8.8\ MeV$ per nucleon.

### 9.5 Why Does Nuclear Fusion Release Energy?

Light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus.

The resulting nucleus generally has a higher binding energy per nucleon.

The difference in total mass is released as energy.

### 9.6 Why Does Nuclear Fission Release Energy?

A very heavy nucleus splits into two medium-sized nuclei.

The resulting nuclei generally have higher binding energy per nucleon.

The mass difference is released as energy.

### 9.7 Atomic Mass vs Nuclear Mass

When using atomic masses, the mass of electrons must be handled consistently.

For a neutral atom:

$M_{atom}=M_{nucleus}+Zm_e$

If atomic masses are used to calculate binding energy, use:

$\Delta m=Zm_H+(A-Z)m_n-M_{atom}$

Where $m_H$ is the mass of a neutral hydrogen atom.

This avoids separately subtracting the electron masses.

## 10. Radioactivity — JEE Extension

### 10.1 Definition

Radioactivity is the spontaneous disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei with the emission of radiation.

Radioactive decay is a nuclear process and is generally unaffected by ordinary changes in temperature, pressure and chemical state.

### 10.2 Types of Radioactive Emission

#### Alpha Decay

An alpha particle is a helium nucleus.

$^4_2He$

General nuclear reaction:

$^A_ZX\rightarrow^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y+^4_2He$

Changes:

- Mass number decreases by 4.
    
- Atomic number decreases by 2.
    

#### Beta Minus Decay

A neutron converts into a proton, an electron and an electron antineutrino.

$n\rightarrow p+e^-+\bar{\nu}_e$

Nuclear reaction:

$^A_ZX\rightarrow^A_{Z+1}Y+e^-+\bar{\nu}_e$

Changes:

- Mass number remains unchanged.
    
- Atomic number increases by 1.
    

#### Beta Plus Decay

A proton converts into a neutron, a positron and an electron neutrino.

$p\rightarrow n+e^++\nu_e$

Nuclear reaction:

$^A_ZX\rightarrow^A_{Z-1}Y+e^++\nu_e$

Changes:

- Mass number remains unchanged.
    
- Atomic number decreases by 1.
    

#### Gamma Decay

An excited nucleus releases excess energy in the form of a gamma photon.

$^A_ZX^*\rightarrow^A_ZX+\gamma$

Changes:

- Mass number remains unchanged.
    
- Atomic number remains unchanged.
    

### 10.3 Properties of Alpha, Beta and Gamma Radiation

|Property|Alpha|Beta|Gamma|
|---|---|---|---|
|Nature|Helium nucleus|Electron or positron|Electromagnetic wave|
|Charge|+2e|-e or +e|0|
|Relative mass|4|Very small|0|
|Penetrating power|Low|Moderate|Very high|
|Ionising power|High|Moderate|Low|
|Deflection in electric field|Yes|Yes|No|
|Deflection in magnetic field|Yes|Yes|No|

### 10.4 Radioactive Decay Law

The rate of radioactive decay is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present.

$\dfrac{dN}{dt}=-\lambda N$

Where:

- $N$ = number of undecayed nuclei.
    
- $\lambda$ = decay constant.
    
- $t$ = time.
    

Solving:

$N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$

Where $N_0$ is the initial number of nuclei.

### 10.5 Activity

Activity is the number of radioactive disintegrations per second.

$A=-\dfrac{dN}{dt}$

Therefore:

$A=\lambda N$

At time $t$:

$A=A_0e^{-\lambda t}$

SI unit: Becquerel (Bq).

$1\ Bq=1\ decay/s$

### 10.6 Half-Life

Half-life is the time required for the number of undecayed radioactive nuclei to become half its initial value.

$T_{1/2}=\dfrac{0.693}{\lambda}$

After one half-life:

$N=\dfrac{N_0}{2}$

After two half-lives:

$N=\dfrac{N_0}{4}$

After three half-lives:

$N=\dfrac{N_0}{8}$

General expression:

$N=N_0\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}$

### 10.7 Mean Life

Mean life is the average lifetime of a radioactive nucleus.

$\tau=\dfrac{1}{\lambda}$

Relationship with half-life:

$T_{1/2}=0.693\tau$

### 10.8 Important Radioactive Decay Results

- Decay constant is independent of the initial number of nuclei.
    
- Half-life is independent of the initial quantity.
    
- Activity decreases exponentially with time.
    
- The same decay law applies to the number of undecayed nuclei and activity.
    
- Radioactive decay is random for individual nuclei.
    

### 10.9 Successive Radioactive Decays

For a decay chain:

$A\rightarrow B\rightarrow C$

The daughter nucleus $B$ may itself be radioactive.

The number of daughter nuclei depends on both the production rate and its own decay rate.

This is important in JEE-level radioactive decay problems.
